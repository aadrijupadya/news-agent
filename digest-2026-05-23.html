Hello.

Gen X has a retirement problem. (And it’s not just them, either.)

A whopping 69% of Gen X workers say they're behind on retirement savings, and 47% say significantly behind, according to a 2025 Bankrate survey. That's the highest share of any working generation right now. 

But this isn’t just about Gen X. Whether you’re planning retirement, approaching it, or already there, we think there are some very important tips and tricks you need to know about… especially if you want to optimize your retirement investments.

So today, we’re sharing some good news about rules around catch-up contributions, Roth conversions, and HSAs that many people don’t know about. Six concrete moves below, each anchored on a real rule and a real-money example.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

1. Max Out the Age-50 Catch-Up

If you're 50 or older, the IRS lets you contribute extra to a 401(k) on top of the regular limit. In 2026, that catch-up is $8,000 per year above the $24,500 base limit for a 401(k), plus $1,100 on top of the $7,500 IRA limit.

Max the 401(k) catch-up alone for 10 years (age 55–65) at 7% average growth and you add about $110,000 to your retirement balance from this one move. And that’s before an employer match!

2. The "Super Catch-Up" Hiding in SECURE 2.0

Most people stop reading at the regular catch-up. They shouldn't. From ages 60 to 63, SECURE 2.0 lets you contribute $11,250 in catch-up dollars to a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), or a thrift savings plan (TSP). That's $3,250 more per year than the standard age-50 catch-up.

The window is closed at both ends. The year you turn 64, you revert to the regular $8,000. Your employer's plan also has to opt in, so confirm before assuming it's available. The extra $3,250 a year for those four years, compounded at 7% to age 65, adds roughly $15,000 to your balance.

3. Roth Conversion Ladder (But Mind IRMAA)

If most of your savings sit in a traditional 401(k) or IRA, you have a tax bomb that detonates at age 73 when required minimum distributions (RMDs) kick in. The common fix: convert pre-tax balances to a Roth IRA in stages through your 50s and early 60s, paying tax now at known rates.

The catch is that the IRS treats a Roth conversion as taxable income for the year you do it, even though you're just moving your own money between two retirement accounts you already own. A $250,000 conversion looks to the IRS like $250,000 of new income on your tax return.

That triggers a Medicare premium surcharge called IRMAA. Retirees with higher annual income pay more for Medicare than retirees with lower income. 

In 2026, crossing $109,000 in income (single) or $218,000 (joint) adds about an extra $974 per year to your Medicare Part B premium, plus a small Part D surcharge. Cross higher thresholds and the surcharges step up sharply. IRMAA uses your income from two years prior, so 2026 conversions affect 2028 premiums.

4. The HSA: The Most Underused Retirement Account in America

If you have a high-deductible health plan (HDHP), a Health Savings Account (HSA) is the rare triple-tax-advantaged account: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for medical expenses are tax-free.

The part most people miss: after age 65, you can withdraw HSA money for any purpose, not just medical. Non-medical withdrawals at 65+ are taxed as ordinary income (like a Traditional IRA), with no 20% penalty. Medical withdrawals stay tax-free forever.

Max the 2026 HSA contribution ($4,400 self / $8,750 family) for 15 years at 7% growth and you end up with roughly $110,000 to $220,000 in a tax-advantaged bucket that doubles as healthcare savings or a retirement supplement.

5. Roll Old 401(k)s Into an IRA to Cut Fees

If you've changed jobs a few times, you probably have old 401(k) accounts sitting at former employers, quietly charging fees. The average 401(k) participant pays an average expense ratio of 0.26% per year, according to a 2025 ICI study.

A self-directed IRA at Vanguard, Schwab, or Fidelity holding broad index ETFs runs closer to 0.03%. That’s a near 9x reduction in annual fees.

On a $200,000 rollover, dropping fees from 0.26% to 0.05% saves about $440 a year. Over 15 years of compounding at 7%, those fee savings add up to roughly $11,000 in additional retirement balance.

6. Don't Ditch Growth Stocks Yet

Conventional wisdom says you should rotate aggressively toward bonds and dividend stocks at 50. 

We'd push back. Vanguard actually recommends staying heavier in stocks longer than the old "100 minus your age” rule of thumb. 

Vanguard’s guidance: keep 90% of your money in stocks through your early 40s, gradually reduce that to 50% in stocks by age 65, and finally start withdrawing at age 72. The reasoning? With a potential 30-year retirement horizon, the bigger risk is outliving your savings, not enduring a 30% drawdown along the way.

If you're already behind on savings, the case for keeping meaningful growth exposure is even stronger. Bond-heavy portfolios cushion volatility, but they may not compound fast enough to close a real gap.

What the Bond Market Is Telling You (Even If You Don't Own Bonds)

Bonds are not exciting. We know. But the bond market is flashing signals right now that stock investors can't afford to ignore.

The 30-year Treasury yield crossed 5% this week for the first time since 2007. The 10-year is hovering around 4.6%. That means the US government has to pay investors over 5% a year just to convince them to lend money for 30 years. Two years ago that number was closer to 4%. The difference? Inflation from the Iran war, a growing federal deficit, and a Fed that might raise rates instead of cutting them.

Why this matters even if you own zero bonds: when yields rise, borrowing costs rise for everyone. Mortgage rates go up. Corporate debt gets more expensive. Companies that need to borrow to grow see their future earnings compress (i.e. profits become relatively smaller), which is why growth stocks and tech names get hit hardest when yields spike.

It also creates competition for stocks. If the government is paying you 5% risk-free, a dividend stock yielding 3% has to work a lot harder to justify its spot in your portfolio.

The flip side: if you're an income investor, this is one of the best entry points for bonds in nearly 20 years. Locking in 5% on a 30-year Treasury is real money. We’re usually looking for something better than 4% (what you can generally earn in a high-yield savings account), so these numbers are immediately interesting to us.

Short-duration Treasury ETFs (like SHV or BIL) are paying north of 5% with almost no interest rate risk. Longer duration (like TLT) pays well but will lose value if yields keep climbing.

The simplest move: if you have cash in a savings account earning 4%, a short-term Treasury or money market fund is probably paying you more right now. That's free money most people leave on the table.

We're not bond guys by nature. But when the bond market starts paying like this while flashing warning signals about inflation, even stock investors should pay attention (in our humble opinion).

International Is Actually Leading the S&P 500 Right Now

Vanguard's total-international ETF (VXUS) is up 10.3% year-to-date at the time of writing. The S&P 500 is behind by more than a whole percentage point. 

For most US-based investors, international has been the part of the portfolio you ignore. That assumption is starting to look out of date. The bigger question now is which markets are doing the actual work.

The Strength Is Concentrated in Three Stories

Japan: corporate governance just got a real shove. The Tokyo Stock Exchange began pressuring listed companies to focus on capital efficiency a number of years ago. Firms trading below book value were specifically asked to publish plans to fix it.

The result: Japanese share buybacks set new records for five straight years and hit 22.32 trillion Yen (roughly $142 billion) in fiscal 2025.

Berkshire Hathaway noticed. Berkshire now owns more than 10% of each of Japan's five major trading houses (Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo, Itochu, Marubeni).

South Korea: the AI memory cycle. One of the best-performing major international ETFs in 2026 is iShares MSCI South Korea (EWY), up 78% YTD and driven largely by Samsung and SK Hynix. Both are critical suppliers of the high-bandwidth memory chips that AI training clusters depend on.

The AI infrastructure trade has been a US-tech story for years. But it’s also a Korean story too.

Brazil: commodity export surge. Favorable terms of trade paired with global demand for natural resources have fueled massive profits for state-owned giants like Petrobras (PBR) and iron ore leader Vale (VALE). As the central bank moves into a major interest rate easing cycle, global capital is flowing back into these heavily undervalued equities.

Where the Bull Case Hasn't Worked

India is the conspicuous disappointment. iShares MSCI India (INDA) is down more than 11% year-to-date in May 2026 after years of being the consensus emerging-market growth story.

The structural case (demographics, GDP growth, China+1 supply-chain rotation) is intact. The problem was valuation. Indian stocks had run to historically high price-to-earnings ratios at their 2024 peak, and investors finally rotated to cheaper markets. This is a valuation reset, not a fundamental break.

Indonesia is in a severe policy crisis. iShares MSCI Indonesia (EIDO) is down nearly 30% year-to-date in May 2026. 

The structural drivers of the prior bull case (the nickel commodity boom, downstream industrialization, expanding consumer tech) are being heavily undermined by sudden state intervention.

What Allocation Actually Makes Sense

Most mainstream guidance (Vanguard, Morningstar) suggests an international allocation somewhere between 20% and 40% of the equity portion of a portfolio.

If you currently hold zero international (which describes a lot of US-focused retail portfolios), the case for adding some exposure now is stronger than it has been in years. The valuation gap is meaningful. The dollar is weakening. Specific stories (Japan, Korea, Brazil) have had real catalysts.

For targeted bets on the strongest international stories, the iShares MSCI Japan ETF and the iShares MSCI South Korea ETF are the most direct routes.

Wrapping up

International stocks are outperforming the US. The strength isn't broad. It's concentrated in a few places.

The long-term picture still favors the US. The shorter-term picture seems to reward diversification in our opinion. If your portfolio currently runs zero international exposure, this is a reasonable moment to revisit that decision.

Cutting Edge AI Research
Perception or Prejudice: Can MLLMs Go Beyond First Impressions of Personality?
This paper evaluates whether multimodal large language models can accurately assess personality traits from images or if they rely on superficial stereotypes and biases. It's significant because it directly tests whether MLLMs exhibit social prejudice similar to humans, revealing a critical limitation in how these models interpret visual information about people.
DelTA: Discriminative Token Credit Assignment for Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards
The paper proposes a method to improve reinforcement learning by identifying which tokens in an LLM's output actually contributed to receiving verifiable rewards, rather than treating entire sequences equally. This matters because it enables more efficient learning from feedback signals, addressing a core challenge in training language model agents through RL.
π-Bench: Evaluating Proactive Personal Assistant Agents in Long-Horizon Workflows
This paper introduces a benchmark for evaluating AI agents that must proactively plan and execute complex, multi-step tasks over extended time periods, moving beyond reactive question-answering. It's novel because it tests whether agents can anticipate user needs and manage long-horizon workflows—a key capability gap for practical AI assistants.
Full Attention Strikes Back: Transferring Full Attention into Sparse within Hundred Training Steps
The paper demonstrates that sparse attention mechanisms, traditionally used to reduce computational costs, can be trained from full attention in just ~100 steps while maintaining performance. This is valuable because it suggests a hybrid approach: use efficient full attention initially, then compress to sparse attention, potentially combining the benefits of both.
ACC: Compiling Agent Trajectories for Long-Context Training
This work proposes methods to compress and organize agent trajectories (sequences of actions and observations) into more efficient representations for training language models on long-horizon tasks. It matters because long-context training is computationally expensive, and better trajectory compilation could enable LLMs to learn from richer, longer agent experiences.
