Hello.

The question we're answering today: what can waiting exactly one year to invest actually cost you?

The intuitive answer is that you lose one year of growth, which sounds like a small thing. In practice, the year you skip could end up being the most expensive year of your investing life. The math is brutal, and the dollar cost gets bigger the longer you wait.

Let's walk through it.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

The Math: $50,000 and One Year of Waiting

Take a $50,000 starting balance. Put it in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. Historically, the S&P 500 has returned roughly 10% per year on average (nominal, with dividends reinvested) since 1928.

We'll use that as our baseline. The usual caveats apply: this is a long-run average, it's before inflation, and any given year can look wildly different.

Investor A invests the $50,000 on January 1, 2026. Investor B also invests $50,000, but doesn't actually pull the trigger until a year later on January 1 of 2027. Then both leave the money alone for the long haul.

Here's where each of them ends up.

Over 20 years:

  • Investor A: roughly $336,000

  • Investor B: roughly $306,000

  • Cost of waiting one year: ~$30,000

Over 30 years:

  • Investor A: roughly $872,000

  • Investor B: roughly $793,000

  • Cost of waiting one year: ~$79,000

That $79,000 figure is pretty brutal. One year of waiting on a $50,000 starting balance ends up costing you more than the entire original investment!

Why the Last Year of Compounding Costs So Much

The reason for those numbers is that compounding is back-loaded. The earliest year of investing matters, but the LATEST year of compounding produces the biggest dollar gain. Your portfolio is at its largest right before you stop, so each percentage point of growth is multiplying a much bigger number.

A 10% gain on $50,000 is $5,000. A 10% gain on $793,000 is $79,000. Same percentage, dramatically different dollar payoff. When you skip a year of compounding, every future year of growth shifts forward by one, so the year you actually lose is effectively the LAST year of your investing horizon. That last year, the one you'd be giving up, is the most valuable one of the entire stretch.

That's why you can't simply "make it up later" by investing for an extra year at the front. The growth math doesn't reverse.

Why People Wait Anyway (and What to Do Instead)

It’s not that people are waiting to invest because they don't believe in compounding. They wait because acting feels riskier than not acting. “The market looks expensive.” “There's a recession on the way.” “The Fed is doing something complicated.” “I’ll start in January when I’ve thought about it more."

These all sound reasonable. But the math stays the same!

So, here are three of the most common reasons why people wait to invest, and what to do about each:

"I'm waiting for the market to drop before I buy in."

Market timing is one of the most expensive habits in individual investing. J.P. Morgan's 2026 Guide to Retirement found that an investor who stayed fully invested in the S&P 500 over the past 20 years saw their portfolio grow roughly 8x. 

The same investor who missed just the 10 best days of that 20-year stretch ended up with roughly half that ending value. That’s wild to us. The catch here is that the best days tend to cluster right next to the worst days, so people trying to dodge crashes typically miss the recoveries too.

That last sentence is everything. We should probably make a bumper sticker out of it. Here it is again: people trying to dodge crashes typically miss the recoveries too. Remember this!

"I want to pay off my high-interest debt first."

Now, before Dave Ramsey fans start getting upset: if you have credit card debt at the current average of roughly 21 to 22% APR, paying that down first will obviously beat almost any market return.

But if your only debt is a home loan at 3-4% interest, the math has historically favored investing while you pay off the mortgage on schedule. (This is a Dave-Ramsey-approved strategy, and he actually suggests doing this! Also, we love Dave Ramsey here, nothing but respect for him.)

"I just don't know what to buy yet."

A broad S&P 500 index fund is the standard starting place. VOO (Vanguard) and SPY (SPDR) are the two most common tickers. You can always reallocate later. Starting "wrong" with a broad index fund still beats starting "right" a year from now.

The Stock: Schrödinger (SDGR)

Schrödinger is one of the more unusual companies you can find on a public market. 

They build physics-based software that simulates how drug molecules behave before any human ever swallows them. Pharma companies pay subscription fees to use that software (Bristol Myers Squibb, Takeda, and all of the top 20 pharma names are customers). The company also runs its own internal drug pipeline and a portfolio of collaborations on top of that software business.

Schrödinger has been around since 1990, but it didn't go public until 2020. Bill Gates is a longtime investor.

The catch is that the stock is down more than 95% from its 2021 highs and currently sits at around a $1 billion market cap. The company is still unprofitable (a $103 million net loss in 2025). In May 2025, management pivoted away from running expensive late-stage clinical trials themselves, refocusing the business on what they're best at: the platform software and earlier-stage drug partnerships. That came with a 7% workforce reduction and roughly $70 million in annual cost savings.

Why it's interesting now: software revenue keeps growing (up roughly 10% in 2025 to about $200 million in annual contract value, with 74% gross margins). The drug-discovery revenue, lumpier by nature, more than doubled in 2025. Management has guided to positive adjusted EBITDA by 2028. The company has roughly $400 million in cash on hand, so there's a runway to get there.

The AltIndex angle: Schrödinger sits at a solid AltIndex score, but the underlying signals are constructive. Sentiment has been stable through a rough stretch for the stock, hiring on the engineering side has held up, and the company has now reported its strongest revenue growth in over a year. The ETF: Invesco Optimum Yield Diversified Commodity Strategy No K-1 ETF (PDBC)

If you want exposure to a basket of commodities (oil, metals, agriculture) without the headache of figuring out which specific commodity to bet on, PDBC is the most popular way to do it. 

The ETF holds futures contracts on 14 commodities. What you get:

  • Diversified exposure to "real things" without picking individual commodities one by one

  • No K-1 tax form at year-end (the fund issues a standard 1099 instead), which avoids one of the bigger annoyances of owning commodity funds

  • About $6.4 billion in assets under management, making it the biggest no-K-1 commodity ETF available

  • An expense ratio of 0.59%, reasonable for an actively managed strategy

But here’s what you give up: commodities are volatile, they don't pay a steady yield like bonds or dividend stocks, and they can have multi-year stretches of going nowhere. PDBC's 5-year returns have been strong (over 50% since 2020), but between 2014-2020, the ETF actually fell by about the same percentage. That’s why we’d say this pick generally works best as a small slice of a diversified portfolio rather than a huge holding.

The case for owning some commodities exposure right now: inflation hedging, the possibility of more crazy geopolitical developments, and the fact that most U.S. portfolios are heavily concentrated in tech stocks that tend to move together. PDBC moves to its own beat.

The Alternative: Platinum

Platinum sit's in an unusual spot. It's both a precious metal (used in jewelry, investment bars, and bullion vaults) and a critical industrial input (catalytic converters in cars, hydrogen fuel cells, electronics, lab equipment). That dual nature makes it a bit more volatile than gold and also harder to pin to a single investment thesis.

2025 was the year platinum's long-running lag finally ended. The metal roughly doubled over the year, broke out above a high it hadn't seen since 2021, and is now in its third consecutive year of running a supply deficit (meaning that mine production isn't keeping up with demand).

Why this is happening:

  • Roughly 70-75% of global platinum supply comes from South Africa, which has had years of power outages and strikes in its mining industry

  • Catalytic converters in vehicles account for around 40% of demand, and the hybrid-vehicle resurgence (electric vehicle adoption has slowed) has propped up that demand

  • Hydrogen fuel cells are a smaller but growing demand source, with platinum as a key catalyst

  • Chinese investment demand for platinum bars and jewelry surged in 2025, with Indian demand also picking up

How to actually own it if you want exposure: most individual investors use PPLT (abrdn Physical Platinum Shares ETF, the largest and most liquid option, with a 0.60% expense ratio) or PLTM (GraniteShares Platinum Trust, slightly cheaper at 0.50% expense). Both hold physical platinum bullion in a vault. Physical coins and bars are an option for those who want to actually hold the metal, but the premiums and storage costs are real considerations.

The catch with platinum after a doubling year: the easy money has been made. The supply deficit is real, but the price has also already moved a lot. We’d suggest treating any new platinum position as a small portfolio diversifier rather than a "back up the truck" trade.
