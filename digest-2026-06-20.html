🤨 What actually makes a dividend “safe” ?

With markets closed today for Juneteenth and the week's relief rally pulling oil and Treasury yields lower, we’re taking today as a strategy session. Specifically, we’re answering the question: where does durable dividend yield come from now?

Since 1960, about 85% of the S&P 500's total return has come from reinvested dividends and compounding, according to Hartford Funds. The yield is not a side dish. It is most of the meal.

The catch is that a high yield and a long history tell you almost nothing about whether a dividend survives the next downturn. Three numbers do most of the work.

First, the payout ratio: how much of earnings goes out as dividends. A range of 40% to 60% is generally healthy, because it leaves room to keep paying when profits dip. REITs and utilities run higher for legitimate reasons, so the bar is sector-specific.

Second, free-cash-flow coverage. Dividends are paid in cash, but earnings include non-cash items, so a company can show a comfortable payout ratio while cash flow barely covers the check. Analysts like to see free cash flow run comfortably above the dividend, and get nervous when it does not.

Third, the balance sheet. When a payout gets cut, debt is usually the trigger, because lenders get paid before shareholders. In 2022, AT&T reset its dividend from 2.08 to 1.11 dollars a share, a roughly 47% cut, after a debt-heavy acquisition left it carrying about 156 billion dollars in net debt. Investors who had chased its 8% headline yield ended up with a smaller dividend and a lower stock price. That is a yield trap, and all three numbers flashed before the cut.

That is why the screen and the math both matter. A Dividend King like Johnson & Johnson, raising its payout for a 64th straight year, has proven it through many recessions. But the streak is the starting point, not the proof. Run these three numbers yourself before you reach for the yield.

Caterpillar benefits when multiple secular trends converge: AI infrastructure buildout, reshoring, grid modernization, and commodity demand. The dealership network spanning 180 countries creates a recurring services revenue stream that competitors struggle to match.

  • The Numbers: Q1 2026 sales hit $17.4 billion, up 22% from the prior year. EPS of $5.54 beat analyst estimates by nearly 20%. The company deployed $5.7 billion in share repurchases and dividends in the quarter alone.

  • The Moat: Construction Industries revenue jumped 38%, and Power Generation sales (which includes data center backup power) surged 41%. The backlog is at record levels, and management described order activity as "strong."

  • The Opportunity: The company's Power & Energy segment is the hidden gem here. Data centers require massive backup power systems, and Caterpillar's large reciprocating engines and turbines are seeing accelerating demand. Meanwhile, the broader infrastructure spending cycle under the IIJA still has years of project timelines to work through.

  • Dividend Yield: 1.7%

  • Biggest Risk: Cyclicality. Caterpillar's business has always been tied to construction and mining cycles. A recession would hit demand, though the record backlog provides some cushion.

Vale (VALE)

Every chip, every cable, every transformer in every data center requires raw materials that somebody has to pull out of the ground. Vale is one of the largest mining companies on Earth, producing iron ore, nickel, and copper at a scale few competitors can match.

  • The Numbers: Vale currently trades at roughly 4.9x forward earnings with a dividend yield approaching 11%. Those are numbers you'd expect from a company in decline, not one sitting on critical materials for the energy transition and AI infrastructure buildout.

  • The Moat: Scale. Vale operates some of the largest and lowest-cost iron ore and nickel operations globally. Nickel demand is expected to grow substantially as battery production expands.

  • The Opportunity: Copper markets remain tight. EVs, grid infrastructure, and data centers all require copper, and new mine development takes a decade. Vale is positioned to benefit from any supply crunch.

  • Dividend Yield: ~11%

  • Biggest Risk: Commodity price volatility and geopolitical exposure. Vale is headquartered in Brazil, and its stock price often moves with iron ore prices and emerging market sentiment rather than company fundamentals.

Bottom Line

The AI revolution requires more than chips. It requires power infrastructure, heavy equipment, and raw materials at unprecedented scale. Eaton, Caterpillar, and Vale represent three different entry points into this physical buildout, each with distinct risk profiles and return potential.

Eaton offers the most direct AI infrastructure exposure but commands the highest valuation. Caterpillar provides diversified industrial exposure with a strong dividend and buyback program. Vale represents deep value with income, though it carries commodity and emerging market risk.

While everyone chases the next AI software play, these companies are executing on record backlogs with real revenue and real cash flow. Sometimes the boring trade is the smart trade.
