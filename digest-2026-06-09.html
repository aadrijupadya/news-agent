The Ceasefire Broke, Then Mended. Here's What It Means for Your Money.

First, what actually happened, per the AP. It unraveled fast. In the days prior, Iran fired missiles and drones at Gulf states, even striking Kuwait's main airport and killing one person, while US and Iranian forces traded fire around the Strait of Hormuz. Then on Sunday Israel struck Beirut's southern suburbs without warning, hitting a residential building and killing two. Iran answered within hours, firing missiles at Israel, its first bombardment since the April ceasefire. Israel said it intercepted them, then hit back with what it called a "large-scale strike on strategic defense systems." By Monday, though, Iran announced an end to its military operations, warning that continued Israeli strikes in Lebanon would bring "far more severe and crushing measures." Trump, who said the Israeli Beirut strike wasn't coordinated with Washington, urged Iran back to the negotiating table.

The part that hits portfolios fastest is oil. Brent crude spiked more than 4% toward $97 a barrel early Monday on the weekend's strikes, before paring much of that jump as Iran signaled it was stepping back. Oil reacts first because of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel off Iran's coast that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. As long as the Strait is in play, every barrel carries a war premium, and you saw that premium inflate and deflate in a single morning.

In plain terms: energy and oil tend to rise when the Strait is threatened (which is why a sliver of energy acts as a natural hedge), gold usually catches a bid, and the broad market sells off into the uncertainty. There's also a knock-on: higher oil feeds inflation, and after Friday's hot jobs report already killed rate-cut hopes, a fresh oil spike gives the Fed even less room to cut. But you also need to be ready for a market rebound in case of a quick new “ceasefire” announcement… like what Iran did today.

Iran has chosen the calmer path (for now), which is exactly why stocks that opened red turned green and oil gave back much of its spike. If the de-escalation holds, that relief continues. If Israel keeps striking Lebanon and Iran follows through on its threat, oil spikes again and the selloff returns.

Today is a perfect example of why panic-selling a scary open is so often a mistake: the investors who dumped into the red gap watched the market climb back over their heads by lunch. The smarter move is knowing where you stand: if you own zero energy and zero gold, this is the kind of event that argues for a small slice of each, not as a war bet, but as insurance. What we'd watch now: whether Israel keeps hitting Lebanon (Iran's stated trip wire), the Strait of Hormuz, and oil.

Apple Finally Shows Its AI Hand. The Market Wasn't Impressed.

Apple unveiled Siri AI at its annual developer conference on Monday, the company's long-delayed answer to ChatGPT and Google's Gemini. The new assistant is more conversational, gets its own dedicated app, and can handle back-and-forth conversations across your iPhone, iPad, and Mac. Apple demonstrated how you can ask Siri when an artist you like is performing nearby, have it set reminders for tour dates, then immediately ask it to play one of their songs. Apple needs Google’s Gemini now.

Cutting Edge AI Research
Your UnEmbedding Matrix is Secretly a Feature Lens for Text Embeddings
This paper reveals that the unembedding matrix in language models can be interpreted as a feature lens that provides interpretable access to semantic information in text embeddings. The finding is novel because it connects mechanistic interpretability with embedding spaces, offering a direct window into what linguistic features models encode without requiring additional probing techniques.
SoCRATES: Towards Reliable Automated Evaluation of Proactive LLM Mediation across Domains and Socio-cognitive Variations
This work presents an automated evaluation framework for assessing how well LLMs can mediate conflicts or sensitive situations proactively across different domains and demographic variations. It matters because reliable evaluation of LLM behavior in high-stakes social scenarios has been missing, and this benchmark enables systematic study of fairness and appropriateness across diverse contexts.
MMAE: A Massive Multitask Audio Editing Benchmark
The paper introduces a comprehensive benchmark dataset for evaluating audio editing models across diverse tasks and editing scenarios. This addresses a critical gap in audio AI evaluation, as audio editing has lacked standardized benchmarks compared to vision and language domains, making model comparison and progress measurement unreliable.
When Tools Fail: Benchmarking Dynamic Replanning and Anomaly Recovery in LLM Agents
This benchmark evaluates how well LLM agents can recover and replan when their tools fail or behave unexpectedly during task execution. It's significant because real-world deployment of agent systems requires robustness to failures, yet most agent research assumes perfectly functioning tools—this work directly addresses that critical gap.
