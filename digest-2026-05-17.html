Hello.

CPI just came in at 3.8% year over year. PPI hit 6%. Brent oil is above $105. And the Fed isn't cutting rates anytime soon.

We're not here to tell you inflation is coming… it’s already here. The question is what to do about it.

Not everyone's portfolio is built for this environment. If you're heavy in long-duration bonds, rate-sensitive tech, or cash earning less than inflation, you might be losing purchasing power every month. So we pulled the historical data on which sectors and asset classes actually perform well when inflation stays elevated, and put together a practical positioning guide.

This isn't macro doomer content. This is just portfolio hygiene!

What History Tells Us

Hartford Funds studied sector performance across every inflationary period since 1973. The takeaway: some sectors reliably outperform during high inflation, and some reliably get crushed. Knowing which is which matters a lot when CPI is sitting at a three-year high.

Equities as a whole outperformed inflation 90% of the time when inflation was low and rising. But when inflation was high and rising? It was basically a coin toss. That's where we are right now.

Here's what works.

Sectors That Historically Outperform

  • Energy. This might be the most direct inflation hedge in the stock market. Energy company revenues are literally tied to oil and gas prices, which are a key component of CPI. With oil above $105 and the Strait of Hormuz disrupted, energy stocks seem to have a floor under them. Names like ExxonMobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX), and ConocoPhillips (COP) all pay dividends while you wait. The Energy Select Sector ETF (XLE) gives you the whole basket. The only drawback is if the US achieves sudden peace in the Middle East… that’s the only case we see where energy comes back down in a big way.

  • Commodities and materials. Gold is near record highs for a reason. It's the oldest inflation hedge in existence. Copper, lithium, and agricultural commodities also tend to rise with inflation because the cost of extracting and producing them goes up. SPDR Gold Shares (GLD) is the simplest gold play. For broader commodity exposure, the Invesco DB Commodity Index (DBC) covers energy, metals, and agriculture in one fund.

  • Consumer staples with pricing power. Companies that sell things people buy regardless of price increases can pass costs through to consumers. Coca-Cola (KO), Procter & Gamble (PG), and Philip Morris (PM) are the kinds of names we look at because their brands are strong enough that people keep buying even at higher prices. The key word here is pricing power. Not all consumer companies have it.

  • Real assets (REITs and infrastructure). Real estate values and rents tend to rise with inflation. Equity REITs have outperformed inflation 66% of the time historically, posting an average real return of 4.6% during inflationary periods. Something like Vanguard Real Estate ETF (VNQ) is the standard play here. Infrastructure (toll roads, pipelines, utilities) also has built-in inflation protection because many contracts have inflation escalators baked in.

  • Banks and financials. This one is counterintuitive, but banks generally benefit from higher interest rates because the spread between what they pay on deposits and what they charge on loans widens. JPMorgan (JPM), Bank of America (BAC), and the Financial Select Sector ETF (XLF) tend to do well in rising-rate environments.

Fixed Income Moves

Bonds are the asset class most directly hurt by inflation, but not all bonds are created equal.

  • TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities). The principal adjusts with CPI, so your purchasing power is protected by design. The iShares TIPS Bond ETF (TIP) is the most popular way to access them. Schwab's research notes that most TIPS yields are positive right now, which makes them decently attractive.

  • Short-duration bonds. Long-duration bonds (10+ years) get hammered when rates rise because their fixed coupons become less attractive relative to new issuances. Short-duration bonds (1 to 3 years) are far less sensitive. Vanguard Short-Term Bond ETF (BSV) or the SPDR Bloomberg 1-3 Month T-Bill ETF (BIL) give you income without the duration risk.

What to Avoid (or Reduce)

  • Long-duration bonds. If rates go higher from here, long-dated Treasuries and bond funds like TLT will continue losing value. Duration is your enemy in this environment.

  • Unprofitable growth stocks. Companies that don't generate cash flow today and promise profits years from now get hit hardest by inflation because higher discount rates reduce the present value of future earnings. This is the "long-duration equity" problem.

  • Cash earning less than inflation. If your savings account is paying 0.5% and inflation is 3.7%, you're losing 3.2% of your purchasing power every year. At minimum, move uninvested cash into a money market fund earning 3.5% or more.

The Bottom Line

We're not predicting where inflation goes next. Nobody knows. But we do know that CPI and PPI are both at three-year highs, oil is above $105 with no ceasefire in sight, and the Fed has zero room to cut rates right now.

If inflation stays sticky for the rest of 2026 (which is looking increasingly likely), portfolios tilted toward energy, commodities, pricing-power staples, TIPS, and short-duration bonds will outperform. Portfolios heavy in long-duration bonds and speculative growth will struggle.

You don't need to overhaul everything. Even small tilts toward inflation-resistant assets can make a meaningful difference. Check your allocation, make sure you're not overexposed to the sectors that suffer most, and let the positioning do the work.

Whatever Happened To…

Tupperware was never just a food container. For a few decades after World War II, it was practically a symbol of American life.

The company emerged in the late 1940s, when inventor Earl Tupper developed lightweight plastic containers with airtight “burping” seals that felt futuristic in an age still dominated by glass jars and wax paper. The products were genuinely impressive by the standards of the day: durable, lightweight, leak-resistant, and far better at preserving food than many existing storage options.

But the real breakthrough came from Brownie Wise, a charismatic saleswoman who realized the product worked best not on store shelves, but in living rooms, and the Tupperware party was born.

Here’s a TV commercial for one .

From the 1950s through the 1980s, Tupperware parties were a cultural phenomenon—part social event, part shopping spree, part escape from household routine. Women would gather on Tuesday evenings, admiring the latest designs:

the iconic Wonderlier bowls , the Jel-Ring mold for fancy Jell-O, the Cake Taker that every mom coveted.

In an era of expanding suburbs and isolated homemakers, the parties offered something bigger than kitchenware. They created community, status, and, for many women, a rare chance at independent income.

Tupperware became a cultural institution because it perfectly matched postwar America: optimistic, consumer-driven, convenience-obsessed, and fascinated by plastic as the material of the future.

But the culture that sustained Tupperware slowly disappeared. As more women entered the workforce, daytime home parties faded. Big-box retail and later e-commerce made direct sales feel unnecessary. Cheap competitors flooded the market. Plastic itself lost its glamorous image and became associated with waste and pollution.

By the 2020s, Tupperware had become more of a nostalgia brand than a cultural force. The company filed for bankruptcy protection in 2024 before reorganizing under new ownership as “The New Tupperware Co.” Today, it is trying to reinvent itself as a leaner digital-era brand, selling products online and through its remaining network of independent consultants in selected global markets.

Still, the brand left a remarkable imprint on American culture. Today, those vintage pastel bowls

are collector's items on eBay . The parties are gone, but somewhere in your kitchen, there's probably still a butter-yellow Tupperware container—lid missing, of course—that refuses to die.
