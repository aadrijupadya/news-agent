Hello.

When people talk about the greatest trades of all time, they usually start with George Soros breaking the Bank of England in 1992 or Michael Burry shorting subprime mortgages starting in 2005. Those are great stories. They also have almost nothing to do with what any of us could have actually done.

Soros needed $10 billion to attack a currency. Burry needed Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank to write him custom mortgage bets that no retail brokerage offered. Most of us don't have those things. We have a brokerage app on our phone.

So today's edition is the version of that list that actually applies. Five stock trades from the past 30 years that any individual investor could have made from a regular brokerage account, no margin or options or institutional access required. 

Each one was boring or hated at the entry point. Each one rewarded patience absurdly well. Each one would have looked like a terrible idea at some point along the way. Let’s dig in.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

What Makes a Great Stock Trade for the Rest of Us

Three things every name below has in common:

  • Anyone could have bought it. No margin, no options, no inside access, no offshore account. A normal U.S. brokerage account did the job.

  • The entry point looked like a bad idea. The company was either unknown, hated, or freshly humiliated. Nobody on cable TV was telling you to buy.

  • The patience required was brutal. Every single name on this list had at least one 50%+ drawdown that a holder had to sit through without selling.

Here are five names that followed that pattern. 

1. Monster Beverage (MNST), mid-1990s onward

In the mid-1990s, the company that's now Monster Beverage was a small California outfit called Hansen Natural that sold juices and sodas. The stock traded for pennies on a split-adjusted basis. In April 2002 they launched a 16-ounce energy drink called Monster, and the company became unrecognizable. They renamed to Monster Beverage in 2012.

$1,000 invested in late 1995 would now be worth more than $8.5 million at current prices, which works out to roughly 8,000x your money over three decades. One of the best-performing names of any kind on a U.S. exchange.

The patience required: 2008 wiped out about 60% of the stock's value after peaking in 2007, and it took more than two years to get back to its prior high. The shareholders who panic-sold at the bottom missed everything that came after.

2. Domino's Pizza (DPZ), late 2008 onward

In November 2008, Domino's stock fell below $4 a share. The pizza was famously rated worse than its competitors' in blind taste tests. Patrick Doyle, then president of Domino's USA (and CEO from March 2010 onward), decided the only way out was to publicly admit it. In late 2009, they launched the "Pizza Turnaround" campaign with ads that flatly said their pizza had been bad and they'd reformulated it from the bottom up.

What followed was one of the great corporate turnarounds in modern history. New recipe, app-based ordering, an entire delivery tech stack, store expansion. The stock peaked at roughly $500 in late December 2021, which works out to more than 100x the November 2008 low.

The patience required: anyone who held through the rebrand watched the stock more than double in a year, then sit flat for months while the market doubted the thesis. Hard to ignore. Easier to sell too early!

3. Texas Pacific Land (TPL), mid-1990s onward

Texas Pacific Land was originally created in 1888 to liquidate the assets of a bankrupt Texas railroad. The "asset" was roughly 3.5 million acres of West Texas land. Over a century, the trust sold most of it off. The company today still owns surface rights to roughly 873,000 acres, plus separate oil and gas royalty interests, all sitting on top of what turned out to be the most prolific oil basin in the United States.

The stock has returned more than 40,000% since the late 1990s. The trust converted to a corporation in January 2021, and TPL was added to the S&P 500 in November 2024.

The patience required: this stock was so obscure that for most of its history it traded by appointment. The investors who owned it weren't watching CNBC. They just held the certificates and let the land do its thing.

4. Chipotle (CMG), January 2006 onward

Chipotle’s initial public offering (IPO) debuted at $22 a share in January 2006, when McDonald's still owned a majority stake. Both companies separated later that year. The stock has compounded at one of the best rates of any restaurant chain in U.S. history.

In October 2015, an E. coli outbreak crippled the company. Same-store sales collapsed. The stock fell more than 40% over the next 18 months and looked like a cautionary tale rather than a great trade. The investors who held through that stretch were rewarded with a multi-year recovery and then a 50-for-1 stock split in June 2024.

From the 2006 IPO to today, on a price-only basis adjusted for the 2024 split, $1,000 at IPO would be worth somewhere around $70,000 (it would have been over $150,000 at the 2024 peak, but the stock has pulled back meaningfully in the last year). One of the best long-run restaurant trades of the modern era.

The patience required: holding through E. coli was the test. Most people didn't.

5. Nvidia (NVDA), January 1999 onward

Nvidia offered its IPO in January 1999 at $12 a share. For the next decade and a half it was a niche gaming GPU company that mattered mainly to PC gamers and certain professional visualization workflows. Nobody outside that world thought about it much.

Then around 2016, the AI boom started, and the chips Nvidia had been making for years turned out to be exactly what the moment needed. The rest is the trade everyone now wishes they'd held. From IPO to today, accounting for six stock splits, Nvidia has returned more than 5,000x your money.

The patience required: in 2002, during the dot-com bust, Nvidia lost roughly 90% of its value. It took more than four years to reclaim its prior high. The number of investors who bought at IPO and held through that drawdown is small.

The Buyback Playbook

A buyback is exactly what it sounds like: a company uses its own cash to buy shares of its own stock from the open market. Those shares get retired, which means every remaining shareholder now owns a slightly bigger slice of the same company.

The catch: not every buyback is a good thing. Companies do them for great reasons and terrible ones, and knowing which is which is most of the game.

GameStop's Buyback (The Good Case)

GameStop authorized a $2 billion buyback yesterday alongside its Q1 results. Net sales was up 14% year-over-year with a record $389.6 million in quarterly net income. The company is sitting on roughly $9.7 billion in cash and liquid investments, most of which it raised by selling its own stock to retail at much higher prices in 2024 and 2025.

With the stock now around $22, they're using some of that cash to buy shares back at a discount. Textbook good buyback: cheap stock, excess cash, no new debt.

Boeing’s Bad Buyback

A good buyback uses excess cash to retire cheap shares. A bad buyback uses borrowed money to buy expensive shares, mostly to mask the dilution coming from employee stock pay. Boeing's $40+ billion of buybacks at high prices between 2013 and 2019, right before they needed every dollar to survive the 737 MAX crisis, is the classic cautionary tale.

What About Google Selling More Stock?

Alphabet went the other direction this week. As we covered earlier this week, the company is raising $80 billion through new stock sales to fund its AI buildout, including $10 billion from Berkshire Hathaway.

When a company issues new stock, your slice shrinks (the technical term is dilution). The bet Google is making is that the new AI compute will eventually pay back the dilution. The longer-term verdict will take years.

The 3-Question Checklist

When you see a buyback announcement, ask:

  1. Is the share count actually shrinking? This is the one that matters most. Pull up the company's diluted shares outstanding over the past three to five years (it's free on any finance site). If that number is steadily falling, the buybacks are real and your slice is genuinely growing. If it's flat or rising despite billions spent "returning cash to shareholders," the buyback might just be mopping up the shares the company hands its employees, and your ownership isn't actually budging.

  2. Is the stock cheap, or near its highs? A buyback after a selloff creates value. A buyback near an all-time high usually destroys it (because they could have bought at a lower price and generated a return instead of probably losing value after the all-time high). Quick gut-check: where does the stock sit in its 52-week range, and is its P/E below its own recent average? Companies are famously bad at timing their own stock, so this is worth ten seconds.

  3. Where's the cash coming from? Excess free cash flow is the green flag (GameStop has a $9.7 billion reserve of cash and could do the stock buyback in all cash if it wants to). But raising debt to fund the buyback is the red flag.

Hello.

The market is at all-time highs. If you've been holding any of the names we've covered over the past few months (Apple, Nvidia, the trillion-dollar names in our Beyond the Mag 7 series), there's a very good chance you're sitting on real gains. Some of those positions have probably grown into bigger chunks of your portfolio than you ever planned for, without you really noticing.

Most of what you read about investing is about when to buy. The art of selling gets way less attention, and it's at least as important. Today's edition is a framework for taking profits in a calculated way: without panic-selling, without hoping the chart keeps going up forever, and without giving the tax bill a heart attack.

First, the disclaimer. This isn't a "sell everything" piece. We're long-term believers in long-term investing. That still involves selling sometimes. The point is to sell for the right reasons.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

Reason 1: Your Original Reason for Buying Has Played Out

Every time you buy a stock, there should be a reason. "It'll go up" doesn't count. A real reason looks something like: "I think this AI chip company will keep growing because they have a multi-year head start, every major cloud company needs their chips, and they keep winning new customers."

The question to ask yourself every quarter or two: is that reason still working?

If a chipmaker you bought has now signed deals with every major cloud company there is, the stock has tripled, and the next leg of growth depends on a brand-new technology that may or may not pan out, that's a moment to consider taking some money off the table. The original story has played out. The next chapter is more of a guess.

If the company still has half its potential customers untouched, the original reason is still working, and there's no real reason to sell yet.

The takeaway: Write down your reason for owning every stock you hold. Re-check the reason every few months. If it's still true, hold. If the story is finished and the next chapter is speculation, consider trimming.

Reason 2: A Position Has Grown Too Big

If you put $5,000 in a stock that's now worth $50,000 and makes up a quarter of your portfolio, congratulations. You've also accidentally made a giant concentrated bet, whether you meant to or not.

Big winners do this all the time. They grow into outsized chunks of your portfolio without you ever making a conscious decision to bet that much on one name.

How to think about it:

  • A common rule of thumb is that no single stock should be more than 5 to 10% of your portfolio unless you have a really strong reason

  • A position that started as 1% of your portfolio and grew to 5% isn't a problem on its own

  • The same position growing to 10%, 15%, or 20% is a real risk that deserves a second look

What to do: Trimming a winner doesn't mean selling all of it. Selling just enough to bring the position back to a normal size (and putting that money to work elsewhere) lets you keep most of the upside without putting your entire retirement on one stock.

Reason 3: You Want Some Cash Ready for the Next Drop

The market has had a long run. Most readers are probably fully invested, with very little cash sitting on the side. There's nothing wrong with being fully invested. But it does mean that when the next market drop comes, you'll have nothing on hand to put to work.

Trimming a chunk of your winners and parking the proceeds in:

  • A high-yield savings account (top notch rates sit around 4%)

  • Short-term Treasuries (similar yield, plus a state tax break if you live somewhere like California or New York)

  • A money market fund

...lets you keep most of your upside while having cash ready for the next time the market hands you a sale. As we covered in the recent 3-bucket strategy edition, having some cash on the side during a market drop is one of the highest-leverage moves an individual investor can make.

Reason 4: Mind the Tax Bill

If you're trimming positions in a regular taxable brokerage account, the IRS wants its piece. Two quick things to know:

Long-term vs. short-term: Holding a stock for more than a year before selling means you pay long-term capital gains tax instead of your regular income tax rate. If you're a few weeks short of the one-year mark on a position you want to trim, waiting can save you real money.

Tax-loss harvesting: If you also have losing positions in the same account, you can sell them at the same time as your winners to offset the gain. The IRS lets you wipe out your full capital-gain bill that way, plus another $3,000 a year against ordinary income.

For positions inside a retirement account (a traditional IRA, 401(k), or Roth), none of this matters at the time of the trade. You can trade freely without owing capital gains tax. (You'll still pay ordinary income tax on traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals when you eventually take the money out in retirement. Roth withdrawals stay tax-free.) That's where most of your active trimming should happen if you can help it.

The Big One: Don't Confuse This With Panic Selling

The framework above is about calculated profit-taking. That's the opposite of:

  • Selling because the market dropped 10% and you got scared

  • Selling because some headline said the world was ending

  • Selling because the stock has been flat for six months and you got bored

Panic-selling at the bottom is one of the most expensive habits in individual investing. The whole point of having a framework is so that you sell for reasons (story played out, position too big, want cash on hand, tax planning), not for feelings.

Cutting Edge AI Research
OCC-RAG: Optimal Cognitive Core for Faithful Question Answering
This paper proposes a method to identify and extract the minimal set of relevant information needed for RAG systems to answer questions accurately, rather than using all retrieved context. By focusing on this 'optimal cognitive core,' the approach reduces hallucinations and improves faithfulness in question-answering tasks, which is critical for making RAG systems more reliable in production settings.
From Activation to Causality: Discovery of Causal Visual Representations in the Human Brain
The paper develops methods to identify which visual features in the human brain causally drive perception, moving beyond correlational neuroscience analyses to establish true causal relationships. This bridges computational neuroscience and AI by revealing how biological neural systems represent visual information in a way that could inspire more robust computer vision models.
KVarN: Variance-Normalized KV-Cache Quantization Mitigates Error Accumulation in Reasoning Tasks
This paper presents a quantization technique for KV-caches in large language models that normalizes by variance rather than magnitude, significantly reducing the numerical errors that accumulate during long reasoning chains. The method is practically important because it enables more aggressive compression of LLM memory without degrading performance on complex reasoning tasks like mathematics and code generation.
World Models Meet Language Models: On the Complementarity of Concrete and Abstract Reasoning
The paper investigates how world models (which learn concrete, predictive representations of environments) and language models (which capture abstract, symbolic reasoning) complement each other in solving reasoning tasks. This work suggests that combining these two paradigms could yield more capable AI systems that excel at both grounded understanding and abstract problem-solving.
Humanoid-GPT: Scaling Data and Structure for Zero-Shot Motion Tracking
This paper demonstrates how to scale motion tracking for humanoid robots using GPT-style language models trained on large amounts of motion data and structural priors about the human body. The zero-shot capability is significant because it enables robots to track and execute novel movements without task-specific fine-tuning, advancing practical robotics applications.
