Apple Finally Shows Its AI Hand. The Market Wasn't Impressed.

Apple unveiled Siri AI at its annual developer conference on Monday, the company's long-delayed answer to ChatGPT and Google's Gemini. The new assistant is more conversational, gets its own dedicated app, and can handle back-and-forth conversations across your iPhone, iPad, and Mac. Apple demonstrated how you can ask Siri when an artist you like is performing nearby, have it set reminders for tour dates, then immediately ask it to play one of their songs. You can also search photos by location and narrow down to specific scenes without opening the Photos app, ask questions about topics buried in your Messages, or highlight multiple files on your Mac and have Siri compare them in a chart. Very cool, very useful features.

But here's what the market noticed: Siri AI is powered by Google's Gemini model. Apple just handed a core piece of its AI strategy to one of its biggest rivals. On the one hand, this means that Apple doesn’t have to spend hundreds of billions on AI research and development, which is huge. Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and the rest of the Magnificent 7 are spending absurd amounts of money on CapEx there. But on the other hand, this is not a partnership you make from a position of strength. Apple needs Google’s Gemini now.

AAPL fell 1.89% on the day. Apple first revealed its AI strategy at WWDC 2024, two years ago. The effort has been plagued by delays and forced Apple to reshuffle its AI leadership. Wall Street was already worried that Apple was falling woefully behind in the AI race, and although Monday's announcement didn’t lead to a massive selloff, it also didn’t lead to investor confidence.

And the timing is making people nervous. Apple's stumble is one more crack in a bull market that some big names are starting to question. On CNBC, Jim Cramer said this is part of why he's turning cautious, naming Apple's weakness, the hot jobs report, and a flood of huge stock supply coming (the record SpaceX IPO prices Thursday, with more AI-related capital raises likely) as reasons "my bullishness can wait."

What to watch now

The real test is adoption and whether users actually upgrade to compatible devices. Apple still has the ecosystem advantage and continues pushing privacy as a key differentiator, with Siri AI running both on-device and in its secure cloud. But if the experience doesn't match ChatGPT's polish or if users don't see enough value to upgrade their hardware, Apple risks becoming the "good enough" AI player instead of the premium one.

Watch how aggressively Apple markets upgrades this fall. If they start discounting older models hard, bundle AI features into every corner of iOS, or lean heavily on financing offers, that's a signal they're scrambling to get the install base onto compatible devices. Also watch whether Apple expands beyond Google's Gemini or stays dependent on a competitor's model. That dependency is the clearest sign of how far behind they really are, and whether they can close the gap before it becomes permanent.

Hello.

The biggest IPO in the history of the stock market prices this Thursday. SpaceX goes public this week, and it's the event everything else is orbiting around.

But notice who isn't celebrating. Bank of America just warned that 7 of its 10 bear-market signals are flashing, near the kind of reading you see at a top. And while retail investors line up for the hottest IPO ever, the most cautious money on the planet, the world's central banks, is doing the opposite: loading up on gold. One crowd is reaching for the rocket. The other is reaching for the lifeboat. Let's talk about both.

The SpaceX IPO Clock Is Ticking

Here's the timeline, and more importantly, what to do about it.

The details: SpaceX is targeting a fixed price of $135 per share for 556.6 million shares, which works out to a valuation north of $1.75 trillion. The roadshow kicked off June 4, pricing is set for after the close on Thursday (June 11), and SPCX is expected to start trading on the Nasdaq Friday (June 12). Prediction market Polymarket is pricing in a roughly 23% first-day pop and a $2.17 trillion close. (OpenAI also confidentially filed to go public this week, completing the SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic triple crown. The IPO floodgates are wide open.)

What "fixed price" actually means for you. Most IPOs are priced through a bookbuilding process where the bankers feel out demand. SpaceX set a fixed $135. That doesn't change the hard truth of IPO investing: the shares at the offer price go to institutions and the brokers' biggest clients, not to retail. Unless your broker is handing you an allocation (most won't) or you snag shares through your brokerage app (good luck), you're probably not buying at $135. You're buying whatever the stock opens at Friday.

Why buying on day one is historically the worst trade.

If Polymarket is right and SPCX pops 23%, that means day-one buyers are paying up around $166 for something that priced at $135. History is not kind to that move. Hot IPOs tend to spike on day one as everyone piles in, then drift lower for months as the hype fades and lockups expire. You rarely have to chase. The smart approach is almost always to let the dust settle, watch a few earnings reports, and decide whether you actually want to own it at a price that makes sense further down the line.

The picks-and-shovels you may already own.

You don't need SPCX to get exposure to the space economy. Public names like Rocket Lab and AST SpaceMobile give you satellite and launch exposure today. The satellite-internet land grab (Starlink versus Amazon Leo) touches names already in plenty of portfolios. And the suppliers and defense contractors feeding the whole buildout are public and investable right now. If your real thesis is "space is the future," you can act on it without buying the most hyped ticker on its most hyped day.

And the bigger picture: not everyone is celebrating.

Bank of America just told clients to take profits, warning that 7 of its 10 bear-market signals are flashing, citing stretched valuations and narrow leadership. And here’s another tell that should make you think: while retail piles into the most hyped IPO in history, central banks have spent the past two-plus years buying gold at a record pace, and it's been one of the best-performing assets in the market. That's not a coincidence! The patient money tends to reach for hard assets exactly when everyone else is reaching for the shiniest, riskiest thing on the screen (and when the US dollar is in question). A trillion-dollar IPO landing into a top-heavy market is the kind of euphoric moment that has capped past bull runs, and a deal this size soaking up that much capital could even hasten a turn.

To be clear, we're not calling a top. We’re just not going to tell you to chase SpaceX at IPO launch.

CPI Came In Hot at 4.2%, but the Core Number Brought Some Relief

The May inflation report tells two stories at once.

Headline CPI rose 0.5% in May and 4.2% over the past year, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. This is the hottest annual reading since April 2023, with energy doing most of the damage as the Iran conflict keeps a war premium baked into oil and gas.

But the part that matters more actually came in cooler. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy, rose just 0.2% on the month, below the 0.3% economists expected, leaving the annual core rate at 2.9%. That's the number the Fed watches most closely, and it came in softer than feared. In plain terms, the headline looks alarming, but it's almost entirely an energy story.

What it means for you

For the Fed, this looks like a "stay put" report, not a "hike rates now" one. A 4.2% headline driven by energy likely doesn’t force the Fed’s hand the way a hot core reading would have, so the path is probably rates on hold through the summer, with 2026 cuts unlikely (unless we get a huge economic slowdown).

Stocks are down on the inflation reading, but not brutally so. For your portfolio, the softer core inflation is why stocks don’t look worse than they do. Long-duration bonds and high-multiple tech are the most sensitive to inflation surprises, and a cooler core also probably takes some pressure off both. Energy still carries the war premium, which is why a small slice of it keeps acting as a natural hedge right now.

What to watch, and what to do

Watch wholesale inflation (PPI) Thursday and the Fed's June 16-17 meeting. High PPI would mean the energy spike is increasing the price of everything else, too; a tame PPI would back up today's "it's mostly just energy" read.

If you've been tempted to bail on bonds, the softer core inflation is a reason to breathe, not to panic. If you own zero energy, a small position earns its keep here as insurance. And if you're in cash, T-bill yields are still elevated because the Fed isn't cutting, so you're being paid to wait. The worst move today might be an emotional one off a scary headline.
