Oracle Has the Biggest Book in Tech. The Bill Is Why the Stock Fell.

Oracle reported record fourth-quarter and full-year results after the bell on Wednesday: quarterly revenue of $19.2 billion (up 21%) and a record $67.4 billion for the year. But the number everyone fixated on was the backlog.

Oracle's remaining performance obligations, the value of signed contracts it hasn't delivered yet, jumped to $638 billion, up $85 billion in a single quarter and up 363% from a year ago. That's future revenue Oracle has booked but not yet earned, and it added more of it in 90 days than it makes in an entire year.

So why did a record quarter send the stock down more than 11%? In a word, financing. Oracle said it expects to raise about $40 billion in fiscal 2027 through debt and equity, including a $20 billion stock sale, on top of $43 billion in debt raised last year, with capital spending running near $55 billion. The market did the math: Oracle is borrowing heavily and diluting shareholders to fund a buildout whose payoff depends on a handful of AI customers, OpenAI chief among them, following through.

What it means for you

Oracle has become one of the purest bets on the AI-infrastructure buildout, and yesterday showed both sides of it. The demand is real and enormous, but fulfilling it means taking on debt, issuing stock, and leaning on customers who are themselves spending far more than they earn. Thus, the 11% drop in ORCL.

The AI buildout is increasingly financed in a circle:

  1. Chipmakers invest in model labs

  2. Labs sign huge cloud contracts

  3. Clouds borrow to buy more chips

  4. Repeat.

The market's message yesterday was that it’s seriously questioning how the AI dream gets financed, not just how big it is.

Note: we’re still bullish longterm on AI, but we’re also paying attention to how the market reacts and responds to these massive AI companies’ financials on a week-to-week basis here.

Moving forward, watch Oracle's capital-spending guidance, how much new debt it actually issues. A backlog is only as good as the customers behind it, so "record backlog" and "rising risk" can both be true at once!

How to Think About the SpaceX IPO

SpaceX is set to begin trading on the Nasdaq tomorrow, at a target valuation of $1.75 trillion. The company plans to raise around $75 billion in the offering, which would make it the largest single IPO in history by a wide margin. 

Most readers are going to face the same question over the next few weeks: should I buy this? The answer will be different for different people based on the exact same data. The point of today's edition is to walk through the three questions worth answering for yourself, not to tell you which way to go.

Three questions: the valuation question, the Elon question, and the IPO question. Let's get into it.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

Question 1: Is $1.75 Trillion a Reasonable Valuation?

Per the S-1 SpaceX filed last month, the company generated $18.7 billion in revenue in 2025. Starlink alone produced $11.3 billion of that, and was nearly 50% bigger than the year before.

The implied valuation math is pretty wild. 

At $1.75 trillion of market cap on $18.7 billion of revenue, SpaceX would be trading at roughly 94 times sales. For comparison, Nvidia (the most expensive Mag 7 stock by this measure) only trades at around 20 times sales. Microsoft and Apple are closer to 10 to 11. A 94x revenue ratio either reflects a real and lasting monopoly on the future of space-based services, or the price is running ahead of the business.

The bull case: Starlink is growing nearly 50% year-over-year and is profitable on a segment basis. The launch business is the dominant player in reusable orbital rockets, well ahead of newer competitors like Blue Origin. And the market opportunity for satellite internet, orbital infrastructure, and Mars cargo runs is enormous.

The bear case: The overall company isn't yet GAAP profitable (adjusted EBITDA is positive, but the headline number is a $4.9 billion loss). The price-to-sales ratio is at "you're paying for a decade of perfect execution" levels. And the biggest new revenue lines (Starlink Mobile, deep-space tourism, Mars infrastructure) are still years away.

Question 2: How Much Weight Do You Put on the Elon Factor?

Betting against Elon Musk over the past 15 years has been one of the most expensive mistakes any investor could have made. 

Tesla has returned more than 300x since its 2010 IPO on a split-adjusted basis. Tesla short-sellers lost an estimated $38 billion in 2020 alone.

That track record matters because SpaceX is more clearly Musk's company than Tesla ever was. He's the CEO, he's the largest shareholder, and the entire strategy (reusable rockets, Starlink, Mars) is built around his personal vision. If you think he keeps being right, the IPO is the first time most individual investors can participate.

The counter-evidence is also real. The Twitter / X acquisition has been a disaster by any normal measure: Fidelity marked its X stake down roughly 70% between Musk's 2022 purchase and late 2024. Tesla itself fell nearly 70% during 2022. Musk's bets work spectacularly when they work, and they fail completely when they don't.

The takeaway: Musk's track record on his "core" technical businesses (SpaceX, Tesla, Starlink) has been remarkable. His track record on the bets outside those (Twitter, the broader political ventures) has been much spottier. The question isn't whether you trust Musk in general. It's how much of SpaceX's valuation is already pricing in continued perfect execution from him.

Question 3: How Do You Think About the IPO Effect?

Some of the best long-term stocks in history have crashed in the months after their IPO before eventually compounding into giants.

The textbook example is Facebook. The company IPO'd in May 2012 at $38 a share. Within four months, the stock had fallen to a closing low of about $17.73 on September 4, 2012, a roughly 54% drawdown. It took about 16 months to reclaim its $38 IPO price. Today, Meta trades around $600 a share, a roughly 16x return from the IPO price. Anyone who panic-sold at the September 2012 low missed every dollar of that.

The mechanism behind this is the 180-day lockup expiration. Most IPOs include a roughly six-month window during which insiders and early investors are restricted from selling. When the lockup ends, a flood of new shares hits the market and the price often takes another leg down. Facebook had five staggered lockup expirations and got pummeled at most of them.

Other examples worth knowing about:

  • Snap crashed below its IPO price within four months and is now down roughly 92% from its 2021 peak.

  • Lyft has never sustainably reclaimed its IPO price.

  • Peloton went from $29 for its IPO and peaked at about $160 in 2021, and now trades around $6.

  • Beyond Meat went from $25 to over $200, and now trades under $1.

What this tells you: even great long-term companies often hand you a much better entry point 6 to 12 months after IPO than on the listing day itself. And some IPOs (the Peloton/Beyond Meat camp) never recover at all.

So... Buy or Skip?

Both answers can be defensible based on the same data. Here's how we'd think about it.

If you're inclined to buy:

  • Keep it to a small slice of your portfolio (5% or less).

  • Be ready to hold through what could be a 30-50% drawdown in the first year.

  • Plan to add more if the stock drops meaningfully without a change in the underlying story.

If you're inclined to skip:

  • Wait until after the 180-day lockup expiration (likely early December 2026).

  • Watch the first few quarters of SpaceX's public earnings reports.

  • A better entry point may show up in late 2026 or 2027 even if the long-term story plays out.

SpaceX's IPO Dream Runs Into Wall Street's Oldest Test

SpaceX priced its IPO at $135 per share on Thursday, raising $75 billion in what's expected to be the biggest public offering in history when shares begin trading Friday. The company has rockets, satellites, Starlink, defense demand, and one of the biggest private-market stories on the planet. What it may not have is the one thing Wall Street eventually asks every growth story to show: that the business can make money. Also, side note, Elon’s mom is at the Nasdaq.

SpaceX's filing shows the scale, but also the gap. The company generated nearly $19 billion in revenue in 2025 but posted a net loss of nearly $5 billion. That gives investors plenty of business to analyze, but not a profitable one yet.

A great company can still be a rough trade on IPO day. After the opening rush, the question shifts from who wants access to what the business can already prove. That's where the data gets uncomfortable.

What it means for you

According to IPO data from the University of Florida's Jay Ritter, money-losing companies had the louder opening act, jumping 26.5% on average on their first day. But three years later, their average return was slightly negative. Profitable IPOs did the opposite: fewer first-day fireworks, better staying power.

SpaceX isn't a tiny startup, and that matters. IPOs with more than $100 million in pre-debut revenue had smaller opening pops but much stronger returns over the next three years than companies below that line. Tech also helps; tech IPOs beat non-tech IPOs after the first close, but the category still needs a real business underneath.

The takeaway is simple: Losses make the IPO pop louder. Sales and tech help. Profits are what matter long term after the confetti clears, either way.
