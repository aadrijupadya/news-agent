The Case for Asymmetric Bets (and 10 Micro Caps the Data Likes)

There's a version of investing advice that says you should never touch a stock under $5. And for most people, most of the time, that's fine advice. Micro caps are volatile, thinly traded, and more likely to go to zero than to the moon.

But we think writing them off completely is a mistake.

Not because we think you should throw 20% of your portfolio into penny stocks. That's how you blow up an account. We're talking about 1% to 5%, max. Money you could light on fire and still sleep fine. That's the starting line for asymmetric bets.

The concept is simple: you're risking a small amount for the chance at an outsized return. You're not betting the house. You're buying a few shots in the chamber, except these shots come with quarterly earnings reports, SEC filings, and CEO interviews you can go watch yourself.

That's what separates micro cap investing. These are public companies. They report their financials just like Apple and Nvidia. You can pull up the balance sheet, look at revenue trends, check insider buying, and make your own call on whether the business has a shot.

The other piece most people miss: you should probably spread your investments around. For example, if you've set aside 3% of your portfolio for small cap bets, putting all of it on one ticker is closer to gambling than investing. Split it across 3, 5, or even 10 names. Some will go to zero. Some will go sideways. But if one or two hit, the math can be worth it.

We're not going to lie, this stuff isn't for everyone. If this level of volatility makes your stomach turn, we totally understand (and you can skip right to the market headlines below). This isn't financial advice, and you should always do your own research and invest based on your own risk tolerance.

The Stock: Granite Construction (GVA)

You may be looking at that ticker right now thinking "what the heck is this stock?" Take a look at the data before writing it off.

Granite Construction is a $6 billion civil construction company based in California. They build highways, bridges, dams, water systems, and rail, the underwhelming-but-massive infrastructure that quietly runs the country. Not exciting, not on most retail watchlists, and up roughly 2.5x over the past five years.

The reason it's interesting now: federal infrastructure spending under the 2021 Bipartisan Infrastructure Law is starting to flow into civil construction in a real way. Granite's contracted backlog sits at a record $6.97 billion, including a recent $495 million Customs and Border Protection contract near Laredo, Texas. And the CEO said the next federal highway bill is likely to come in significantly higher than the original bipartisan statute, according to Construction Dive.

The dividend is small (well under 1%), so this isn't an income play. Think of it as a quiet value-and-growth bet on the U.S. spending the next decade rebuilding the country's physical infrastructure.

The ETF: JPMorgan Nasdaq Equity Premium Income (JEPQ)

If you want Nasdaq exposure but less volatility than holding QQQ directly, JEPQ is one of the cleanest options available. The fund holds a Nasdaq-100-like portfolio and sells covered calls against it, generating monthly cash distributions from the option premiums.

What you get:

  • Around 10–11% trailing distribution yield, paid monthly

  • About $38 billion in assets, making it one of the largest covered-call ETFs in the world

  • A 0.35% expense ratio, which is reasonable for an actively managed strategy if that’s your thing

  • Active management that adjusts which calls to write based on current implied volatility

The trade-off: in a runaway bull market, the covered calls cap your upside. In essence, you're swapping some growth upside for predictable monthly income. Also, if you’ve been reading us for a while, you know that we usually go for minimum fees and usually opt for index funds instead of managed funds as a result; this is an exception because of the covered calls.

Anyway, this may be a good pick for investors who want Nasdaq exposure but expect choppy or sideways markets, or those building a paycheck-style portfolio inside a Roth IRA or 401(k) where the ordinary-income distributions don't create a tax drag.

The Alternative: Private Credit Interval Funds

Private credit refers to loans made by non-bank lenders (asset managers, specialty finance firms) to private, mid-sized companies, with yields higher than bank loans because lenders take on more credit risk and provide less-liquid capital. Also, yes, we know that private credit is looking a little dicey in the headlines right now. We cover that below!

Interval funds are how retail investors typically access this asset class. They're closed-end funds that allow new investment daily but only offer redemption windows on a set schedule. You get institutional-style lending exposure with limited but real liquidity.

The retail flagship is the Cliffwater Corporate Lending Fund (CCLFX). Launched in 2019, it holds primarily senior secured first-lien loans, has produced high single-digit to low double-digit annualized returns historically, and pays quarterly distributions. Most retail investors reach it through an RIA platform rather than directly.

Is private credit in dire straits right now? Tricolor Holdings and First Brands filed for bankruptcy in late 2025, both with hidden leverage and questionable collateral, and lenders are repricing. Blackstone has tightened redemption caps; Blue Owl restricted withdrawals from a retail-focused fund. The headlines are real.

But the defaults that have surfaced look idiosyncratic and fraud-driven rather than a broad credit cycle turning. The asset class still pays you well to lend to companies that don't have access to public bond markets. Of course, it’s up to you in the end, and you should always do your own research!

Hello.

Gen X has a retirement problem. (And it’s not just them, either.)

A whopping 69% of Gen X workers say they're behind on retirement savings, and 47% say significantly behind, according to a 2025 Bankrate survey. That's the highest share of any working generation right now. 

But this isn’t just about Gen X. Whether you’re planning retirement, approaching it, or already there, we think there are some very important tips and tricks you need to know about… especially if you want to optimize your retirement investments.

So today, we’re sharing some good news about rules around catch-up contributions, Roth conversions, and HSAs that many people don’t know about. Six concrete moves below, each anchored on a real rule and a real-money example.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

1. Max Out the Age-50 Catch-Up

If you're 50 or older, the IRS lets you contribute extra to a 401(k) on top of the regular limit. In 2026, that catch-up is $8,000 per year above the $24,500 base limit for a 401(k), plus $1,100 on top of the $7,500 IRA limit.

Max the 401(k) catch-up alone for 10 years (age 55–65) at 7% average growth and you add about $110,000 to your retirement balance from this one move. And that’s before an employer match!

2. The "Super Catch-Up" Hiding in SECURE 2.0

Most people stop reading at the regular catch-up. They shouldn't. From ages 60 to 63, SECURE 2.0 lets you contribute $11,250 in catch-up dollars to a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), or a thrift savings plan (TSP). That's $3,250 more per year than the standard age-50 catch-up.

The window is closed at both ends. The year you turn 64, you revert to the regular $8,000. Your employer's plan also has to opt in, so confirm before assuming it's available. The extra $3,250 a year for those four years, compounded at 7% to age 65, adds roughly $15,000 to your balance.

3. Roth Conversion Ladder (But Mind IRMAA)

If most of your savings sit in a traditional 401(k) or IRA, you have a tax bomb that detonates at age 73 when required minimum distributions (RMDs) kick in. The common fix: convert pre-tax balances to a Roth IRA in stages through your 50s and early 60s, paying tax now at known rates.

The catch is that the IRS treats a Roth conversion as taxable income for the year you do it, even though you're just moving your own money between two retirement accounts you already own. A $250,000 conversion looks to the IRS like $250,000 of new income on your tax return.

That triggers a Medicare premium surcharge called IRMAA. Retirees with higher annual income pay more for Medicare than retirees with lower income. 

In 2026, crossing $109,000 in income (single) or $218,000 (joint) adds about an extra $974 per year to your Medicare Part B premium, plus a small Part D surcharge. Cross higher thresholds and the surcharges step up sharply. IRMAA uses your income from two years prior, so 2026 conversions affect 2028 premiums.

4. The HSA: The Most Underused Retirement Account in America

If you have a high-deductible health plan (HDHP), a Health Savings Account (HSA) is the rare triple-tax-advantaged account: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for medical expenses are tax-free.

The part most people miss: after age 65, you can withdraw HSA money for any purpose, not just medical. Non-medical withdrawals at 65+ are taxed as ordinary income (like a Traditional IRA), with no 20% penalty. Medical withdrawals stay tax-free forever.

Max the 2026 HSA contribution ($4,400 self / $8,750 family) for 15 years at 7% growth and you end up with roughly $110,000 to $220,000 in a tax-advantaged bucket that doubles as healthcare savings or a retirement supplement.

5. Roll Old 401(k)s Into an IRA to Cut Fees

If you've changed jobs a few times, you probably have old 401(k) accounts sitting at former employers, quietly charging fees. The average 401(k) participant pays an average expense ratio of 0.26% per year, according to a 2025 ICI study.

A self-directed IRA at Vanguard, Schwab, or Fidelity holding broad index ETFs runs closer to 0.03%. That’s a near 9x reduction in annual fees.

On a $200,000 rollover, dropping fees from 0.26% to 0.05% saves about $440 a year. Over 15 years of compounding at 7%, those fee savings add up to roughly $11,000 in additional retirement balance.

6. Don't Ditch Growth Stocks Yet

Conventional wisdom says you should rotate aggressively toward bonds and dividend stocks at 50. 

We'd push back. Vanguard actually recommends staying heavier in stocks longer than the old "100 minus your age” rule of thumb. 

Vanguard’s guidance: keep 90% of your money in stocks through your early 40s, gradually reduce that to 50% in stocks by age 65, and finally start withdrawing at age 72. The reasoning? With a potential 30-year retirement horizon, the bigger risk is outliving your savings, not enduring a 30% drawdown along the way.

If you're already behind on savings, the case for keeping meaningful growth exposure is even stronger. Bond-heavy portfolios cushion volatility, but they may not compound fast enough to close a real gap.

Cutting Edge AI Research
Mega-ASR: Towards In-the-wild² Speech Recognition via Scaling up Real-world Acoustic Simulation
This paper improves automatic speech recognition by scaling up realistic acoustic simulations to train models on diverse, real-world audio conditions rather than relying solely on clean recorded data. This approach is significant because it addresses the long-standing domain gap problem in ASR—models trained on synthetic or clean data often fail on messy real-world audio with background noise, accents, and room acoustics.
Video2GUI: Synthesizing Large-Scale Interaction Trajectories for Generalized GUI Agent Pretraining
This paper generates large-scale synthetic interaction trajectories from videos to pretrain GUI agents that can perform computer tasks by understanding visual interfaces and executing actions. This is novel because it solves the data scarcity problem for GUI agents—previously hard to obtain—by automatically synthesizing training data from video, enabling more generalized agents that work across diverse applications.
OScaR: The Occam's Razor for Extreme KV Cache Quantization in LLMs and Beyond
This paper presents a method to aggressively compress the key-value cache in large language models through quantization while maintaining output quality. This matters because KV cache is a major memory bottleneck during LLM inference; extreme compression directly enables longer context windows and faster inference on resource-constrained hardware.
You Only Need Minimal RLVR Training: Extrapolating LLMs via Rank-1 Trajectories
This paper shows that LLMs can be effectively enhanced through reinforcement learning with very minimal training using low-rank trajectory representations instead of full policy updates. This is significant because it dramatically reduces the computational cost of RLVR training while maintaining or improving performance, making reasoning enhancement practical at scale.
Toto 2.0: Time Series Forecasting Enters the Scaling Era
This paper demonstrates that scaling up model size and data in time series forecasting yields significant improvements, challenging the previous assumption that traditional statistical methods were sufficient for this task. This is impactful because it shows that modern deep learning scaling principles successfully transfer to time series, opening a new research direction and improving forecasting accuracy across domains.
