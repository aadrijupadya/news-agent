Start With the Emotional Math, Not the Financial Math

To begin: the fact that this money came (or is going to) someone you love, and rushed decisions made in the months after a loss could lead to regret.

Behavioral research shows that people treat windfall money differently than money they earn. Inherited money is especially prone to two failure modes: spending it quickly to "honor" the deceased (often through buying things they would have liked) or sitting on it in cash for years out of guilt about benefiting from the loss. Both are normal grief responses, but both can also hurt your long-term finances if they go on too long.

Grief affects financial decision-making more than most people realize. Big moves made in the first few months after a loss tend to feel right at the time and look wrong a year later.

What we would do:

  • Park the money somewhere safe and earn a reasonable yield (around 4% in a high-yield savings account or short-term Treasury fund) for at least 6 months (this is a classic piece of advice from the Bogleheads!). Financial planners commonly recommend 6 to 12 months.

  • Use that time to figure out exactly what you've inherited. An IRA, a brokerage account, real estate, and a life insurance payout all carry different rules.

  • Avoid any decision that locks the money up for years until you've had time to think clearly. A person going through grief is not always the best at evaluating long-term commitments.

Lump Sum or Spread It Out Over Time?

Once you're ready to actually invest, the biggest practical question is whether to put the whole inheritance in the market at once, or spread it out in chunks over months.

What does the math say? Well, it says lump sum wins more often than not (though we’re not blanket-statement suggesting that you do that). A 2012 Vanguard study analyzing decades of U.S., U.K., and Australian market data found that investing a lump sum immediately beat spreading it out over 12 months in roughly two-thirds of historical periods. 

The logic is thatmarkets have historically gone up more often than they've gone down, so any delay in getting invested usually costs you growth.

So, if want to, you could totally go for this option and chances are that your investment will perform better than it would if you spread out the investment over time.

However, it might be better to spread it out.The case for this option mostly emotional rather than mathematical. Spreading it out (often called "dollar-cost averaging") means you're never fully invested right before a crash, and also that you don’t have to stomach the intensity of one huge investment all at once. That feeling is real, and it's worth factoring into your financial decision making, even it it might technically lose to lump-sum investing.

What we would do:

  • If the inheritance is small enough relative to your overall retirement portfolio that a 30% drop in the market wouldn't change your long-term plan, maybe lump-sum invest it.

  • If the size of the inheritance is large enough that a 30% drop would genuinely set you back (or would be too stressful mentally), spread it out over 6 to 12 months. The emotional protection might be worth the percentage points you’d potentially lose!

The Tax Rule You Cannot Ignore: The 10-Year Rule for Inherited IRAs

If part of what you inherited is a traditional IRA or 401(k), there is one rule that matters more than any other. Most non-spouse beneficiaries who inherited a retirement account after January 1, 2020 must withdraw the full balance within 10 years.

Distributions from a traditional inherited IRA are taxed as ordinary income. If you inherit a $500,000 traditional IRA and don't plan your withdrawals carefully, you can end up with a five- or six-figure tax bill just from poor timing. Spreading those withdrawals strategically across the decade can save you thousands by keeping you out of higher tax brackets.

Here's the part that trips people up. Whether you can withdraw on your own schedule depends on one thing: whether the person you inherited from had already started taking their required minimum distributions (RMDs).

If they passed away before they were required to start RMDs, you have full flexibility. You can take the money all at once at the end, evenly across the decade, or front-loaded, as long as the account is empty by year 10.

If they passed after they had started taking RMDs, the rules are stricter. You're required to take a minimum distribution every year in years 1 through 9, and then empty whatever's left by year 10. Skipping one of those annual withdrawals can trigger a penalty of up to 25% of the amount you were supposed to take, so this is not one to wing.

What to do:

  • Identify your beneficiary type first. Spouses, minor children, disabled beneficiaries, and other "eligible designated beneficiaries" get different rules entirely.

  • Find out whether the original owner had started their RMDs. That single fact determines whether you have a free hand or a required-withdrawal schedule.

  • Either way, if you're a non-spouse subject to the 10-year rule, sketch out a withdrawal plan that spreads the taxable income across multiple years instead of getting hit with a giant bill at the end.

  • Inherited Roth IRAs follow the same 10-year drain rule, but the distributions are tax-free, so the timing matters far less.

The Tax Rule That Works In Your Favor: Step-Up in Basis

For most non-retirement assets (stocks in a brokerage account, real estate, taxable mutual funds), there's a tax provision called "step-up in basis" that can save heirs an enormous amount of money in capital gains taxes.

Without step-up in basis, you'd owe taxes on every dollar of appreciation since the original owner bought the asset. With it, you only owe taxes on appreciation that happens after the date of death. For assets held for decades, this is the single biggest tax break in the inheritance system.

The catch? Step-up in basis only applies to non-retirement accounts. Inherited IRAs do NOT get a step-up. Real estate, brokerage holdings, and most other non-retirement assets do.

What to do:

  • Get an official date-of-death valuation for any non-retirement assets you inherit. That becomes your new cost basis for tax purposes.

  • If you plan to sell inherited assets, doing it sooner (rather than letting them appreciate for years afterward) usually minimizes the capital gains you'd owe on any post-death appreciation.

Three things to anchor on if you're investing inherited money:

  1. Don't rush. Park the money safely for at least 6 months before making any large, irreversible move.

  2. Know the tax rules. The 10-year rule on inherited IRAs is the biggest landmine. Step-up in basis is the biggest gift.

  3. Lump sum usually wins. Unless a 30% market drop would genuinely set back your long-term plan, the math favors getting the money invested rather than spreading it out.

The money came from someone who built it over decades and chose to leave it to you. The best way to respect that is to give yourself enough time to make decisions you'll still endorse five years from now.

Whatever Happened To…

It started with a single British penny. When the world's first adhesive postage stamp, the "Penny Black," bearing a profile of Queen Victoria, went on sale in May 1840, people began hoarding them almost immediately. (You can buy one in mint condition today on eBay for about $4,000).

Within a decade, collecting had spread across Europe and to America, and by the 1880s, an estimated 25,000 U.S. enthusiasts were already cataloguing and trading. In 1893, the first U.S. commemorative series, honoring the World Columbian Exposition, sent interest soaring further. A new hobby had found its audience.

By the early 1970s, philately wasn't just a hobby—it was practically a civic institution. The U.S. Postal Service estimated that 20 million Americans collected stamps, nearly one in 10 people. Kids soaked them off envelopes, catalogued them in albums, and traded them at school.

The USPS leaned in hard, issuing splashier commemorative designs and even declaring October "National Stamp Collecting Month" in 1981. And collectors developed different styles of holding and displaying their treasure, as this video shows .

Then the bubble popped. As the collector base swelled through the late '70s, speculators piled in, and stamp values doubled year over year between 1977 and 1980. When that frenzy collapsed, it left a bad taste.

The organized hobby kept growing—the American Philatelic Society peaked at nearly 58,000 members in 1988—but decline had set in. What followed was a slow bleed that the philately hobby couldn't survive. Email killed the stamped letter. Self-adhesive stamps cut off the easiest entry point for new collectors. The Junior Philatelic Society of America folded entirely. A generation grew up and a stamp album felt about as relevant as a butter churn.

The APS today counts roughly 23,000 members, less than half its 1988 peak, and many collections assembled over decades are worth a fraction of their former value on resale.

Two bright spots remain. At the ultra-rare end, serious money still trades—a single 1856 British Guiana stamp fetched $9.5 million at Sotheby's in 2017. Here’s a list of the 10 most valuable U.S. stamps .

SK Hynix

SK Hynix is the Korean memory-chip giant that supplies most of the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) inside Nvidia's AI accelerators. If Nvidia's GPUs are the brains of the AI buildout, SK Hynix is the company shipping the brains' working memory. The stock is up roughly 250% year-to-date and crossed $1 trillion in market cap for the first time last week.

Why it matters right now:

  • They are Nvidia's lead HBM partner, and whoever wins this generation of memory tends to win the next several.

  • HBM pricing has expanded SK Hynix's margins meaningfully, and legacy DRAM has also rebounded as memory makers shifted capacity toward HBM production.

  • Korean equities have re-rated higher on the back of Korean government corporate-governance reforms.

The bull case: AI memory demand is still in early innings. SK Hynix's pricing power on HBM is structural rather than purely cyclical.

The bear case: Memory has historically been one of the most cyclical industries in tech. When the AI capex cycle eventually slows, even the best supplier sees pricing crack.

Micron Technology (MU)

Micron is the U.S. version of the same story. The stock rocketed 19% in a single session last week after UBS tripled its price target (from $535 to $1,625), pushing Micron over the $1 trillion mark for the first time.

Why it matters right now:

  • It's the only U.S.-listed pure-play HBM maker, which carries a strategic premium versus the Asian competition.

  • CHIPS Act benefits flow into Micron in the form of manufacturing subsidies for their Idaho and New York fabrication plants.

  • The demand outlook has shifted from "cyclical concerns" to "supply-constrained for the foreseeable future".

The bull case: Same as SK Hynix, with an added geopolitical premium for being U.S.-based.

The bear case: Tripling a price target in a single research note is typically a backward-looking momentum call rather than a forward-looking thesis. If memory demand cools, the multiple resets hard.

Visa (V)

Visa is the company most investors think of as a credit card, when it's really a toll road. Visa doesn't lend you money or take on credit risk. It runs the network that moves money between your bank and the merchant's bank, and takes a tiny cut of nearly every swipe. That toll-road model is why Visa runs operating margins north of 60%, making it one of the most profitable large companies on Earth. The market cap sits around $615 billion, which is huge, but still less than half of Walmart and a fraction of the Mag 7 giants. That's exactly why it belongs in this series.

Why it matters right now:

  • Visa processes more than 70% of the almost $5 trillion in annual US debit card transactions, according to the DOJ. It's a tollbooth on a massive and growing share of global commerce.

  • The business grows almost automatically as the world shifts from cash to digital payments and as cross-border travel spending climbs.

  • Visa is moving early on AI commerce. Its Visa Intelligent Commerce platform completed hundreds of AI-agent-initiated transactions in late 2025, with more than 100 partners building on it. If AI agents start doing your shopping, Visa wants to be the rails they pay on.

The bull case: Visa is a near-toll-booth on global spending with margins most companies can only dream of. The cash-to-digital shift still has years to run, especially overseas, and agentic AI payments could open an entirely new lane of transaction volume.

The bear case: The DOJ is suing Visa for monopolizing US debit, and a judge cleared the case to proceed in June 2025. Regulators worldwide keep eyeing the fees Visa charges, and new real-time payment rails and stablecoins are a long-term threat to the toll road itself.

One more thing worth knowing about Visa: it's a steady dividend grower, having raised its payout every year since going public in 2008 (17 years!). And the board just hiked the dividend 14% to $0.67 per share in late 2025. The yield is small because the stock has compounded so much, but the growth of the payout is what income investors actually care about here.
