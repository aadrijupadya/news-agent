This Past Weekend

Two things happened over the past few days that don't usually share a calendar: the most dangerous conflict of the year moved toward peace, and the biggest IPO in history hit the market. Both pushed the same way. And that’s why markets are very, very green this morning.

Cleaning Up an Oily War?

Start with the war. On Sunday, the US and Iran announced a deal to end the fighting. It extends the ceasefire for 60 days while the two sides work toward a permanent end, with a formal signing set for Friday in Switzerland and the Strait of Hormuz reopening once it's signed. President Trump went further, declaring the deal complete and the Strait already open.

The reaction was immediate. Oil fell about 4% to its lowest level in three months, down roughly 13% from the middle of last week. That matters far beyond energy stocks. The war premium in oil was the single biggest driver of the hot CPI and PPI inflation readings we covered last week. If crude stays down, that inflation scare fades, the Fed gets breathing room, and the pressure that was building on stocks lets up. That's why equities jumped.

One caveat: this is a 60-day extension and a framework, not a signed permanent peace. If the signing slips or the deal cracks, the premium snaps right back. We're watching Friday’s scheduled peace deal signing.

Up Up and Away

Then there's SpaceX, which went public Friday in the largest IPO ever, raising about $75 billion (more below). The bigger point is that a deal that size pulls a wall of cash out of the market, the exact "supply" worry the bears flagged… But stocks rose anyway. When a record IPO lands and peace breaks out in the same 72 hours, the market forgives a lot. What’s interesting is that SpaceX is up 8.5% today along with the rest of the market with no signs of slowing. We’ll see if that momentum holds up all week.

Our take is that SpaceX might be an amazing long-term hold, but we wouldn’t be surprised if the stock falls significantly in the coming months as Musk and co. adjust to being a publicly traded company.

For your portfolio: lower oil, cooler inflation, and a risk-on tape, with one peace deal signature on Friday standing between total relief and a major relapse.

OmniDirector: General Multi-Shot Camera Cloning without Cross-Paired Data
This paper presents a method for cloning camera movements and angles across multiple video shots without requiring paired training data of different camera perspectives. The approach is novel because it solves a practical video production problem—synthesizing consistent cinematography—without the expensive requirement of collecting aligned multi-view footage, making it immediately applicable to film and animation workflows.
Memory is Reconstructed, Not Retrieved: Graph Memory for LLM Agents
The paper proposes storing LLM agent memories as reconstructible graph structures rather than retrieving pre-stored vectors, allowing agents to dynamically regenerate relevant context on-demand. This is significant because it addresses a fundamental limitation of current retrieval-augmented systems: static memory retrieval can miss emergent connections between facts that only become relevant when queried in specific contexts.
From Chatbot to Digital Colleague: The Paradigm Shift Toward Persistent Autonomous AI
This work articulates a vision shift from one-shot chatbot interactions to persistent AI agents that maintain continuity, learn from experiences, and operate autonomously over extended periods. It matters because it identifies and formalizes what many researchers are pursuing separately—the architectural and behavioral requirements for AI systems that function as reliable long-term collaborators rather than stateless question-answerers.
APPO: Agentic Procedural Policy Optimization
The paper introduces a training algorithm that optimizes policies for procedural tasks where agents must execute multi-step workflows, learning both decision-making and action sequencing. This is novel in combining reinforcement learning with the structured nature of procedural tasks (recipes, code generation, etc.), potentially improving how AI systems handle complex, ordered instructions.
Rethinking RAG in Long Videos: What to Retrieve and How to Use It?
This paper investigates optimal retrieval strategies for long-form video understanding, questioning what video segments should be retrieved and how to best integrate them for question-answering. The contribution is timely and concrete: it identifies that naive temporal retrieval from long videos often fails, and proposes semantic-based selection methods that significantly improve performance on video reasoning tasks.
