Hello.

Not every investment needs to be a growth bet. Some just pay you.

Kinder Morgan yields 4% and just posted its best quarter in years. BlackRock's new Bitcoin income ETF targets 15-25% annual distributions. Gladstone Land pays monthly dividends from farmland rent. Three different assets, three different income streams, zero overlap with the Mag 7.

The Stock: Kinder Morgan (KMI)

Kinder Morgan just delivered what CEO Kimberly Dang called "the best quarter I can remember," and the stock barely moved.

The company operates 78,000 miles of pipeline and 136 terminals across North America, with about two-thirds of revenue from natural gas. In Q1 2026, adjusted EBITDA rose 18% to $2.54 billion while adjusted EPS jumped 41%.

The angle most investors are missing: natural gas demand is surging because data centers need power. Kinder Morgan's internal forecast projects 150 Bcf/day of U.S. demand by 2031, up 27% from current levels. Executive Chairman Richard Kinder put it simply: "The natural gas story has legs."

The numbers:

  • Dividend yield: ~4%, paid quarterly

  • Project backlog: $10.1 billion, 90%+ weighted toward natural gas

  • Leverage ratio: 3.6x net debt to EBITDA (lowest since 2014)

  • Credit rating: Moody's upgraded to Baa1 this quarter

Main opportunity: The backlog. Three major pipeline expansions are scheduled to come online between Q1 2027 and late 2028, converting committed capital into contracted EBITDA that current estimates haven't fully captured.

Main risk: Commodity cycles and regulatory headwinds. If natural gas prices crater or the energy transition accelerates faster than expected, the growth thesis weakens.

The ETF: iShares Bitcoin Premium Income ETF (BITA)

BlackRock just launched a Bitcoin ETF that pays you to hold it.

BITA holds spot Bitcoin and shares of BlackRock's flagship IBIT fund, then sells covered call options on roughly 25-35% of its IBIT holdings. The option premiums get distributed to investors monthly. Bitcoin doesn't generate yield natively (no staking like Ethereum), so this is one of the few ways to get income from BTC without selling it.

The numbers:

  • Target yield: 15-25% annualized, variable depending on volatility

  • Upside capture: Aims for ~70% of Bitcoin's price appreciation

  • Expense ratio: 0.65% (higher than IBIT's 0.25%, lower than competitors like YBTC at 0.99%)

  • Tax treatment: 60% long-term / 40% short-term on option premium gains

The trade-off: Covered calls cap your upside. In a face-ripping rally, BITA will lag pure spot exposure because BlackRock has to deliver shares at the strike price. You're swapping some upside for monthly income.

BlackRock Head of Digital Assets Robert Mitchnick said, "A significant segment of our client base is interested in bitcoin but is also highly focused on yield generation."

Who this is for: Investors who want Bitcoin exposure but expect choppy or sideways markets over the next year. If you think Bitcoin is headed to $150K by December, you probably want straight IBIT instead.

The Alternative: Farmland REITs

Here's an asset class that doesn't care about Jerome Powell, tariff announcements, or whether Nvidia beats earnings: dirt.

Farmland has historically delivered returns between 10-12% annually over multi-decade periods. It's also one of the few assets that benefits directly from inflation, since food prices tend to rise alongside everything else. Most retail investors can't buy a farm. Farmland REITs solve that.

Gladstone Land (LAND) is the purest play. The company owns farms across 14 states, leasing to farmers who grow fruits, vegetables, and nuts. The stock yields around 6%, paid monthly, with 35 dividend raises over the past 39 quarters. More than half of its parcels are in California, where irrigated cropland has increased in value by 260% over 25 years.

Farmland Partners (FPI) takes a similar approach but focuses more on row crops like corn, soybeans, and wheat. Larger by acreage, lower valuation, smaller yield.

Why it works:

  • Inflation hedge: When food prices rise, farmland values and rental rates follow

  • Low correlation: Farmland returns move independently of stock and bond markets

  • Scarcity: They're not making more farmland

  • Steady demand: People eat regardless of what the economy does

The risks: Farmland REITs are less liquid than equity REITs. They're sensitive to interest rates, weather, and commodity cycles. LAND has been volatile as rising rates compressed valuations across the REIT sector.

The appeal is diversification. Farmland income has nothing to do with AI hype, Fed meetings, or geopolitical drama. It's rent from people growing food.

Bottom Line

Kinder Morgan, BITA, and farmland REITs don't usually show up in the same conversation. One moves natural gas, one sells call options on Bitcoin, and one collects rent from farmers.

But they share a common thread: each generates income from a real underlying activity. Pipeline fees, option premiums, and farm rent are cash flows that exist whether the broader market goes up, down, or sideways.

Hello.

Almost every investor has done this at some point. A stock you bought drops, and instead of selling, you decide to wait until it gets back to what you paid. Then you'll close the position. Take your money. Move on.

Then you wait. And wait. Sometimes for years.

It feels emotionally reasonable in the moment, but the math definitely says otherwise. "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid" is one of the most expensive sentences in investing, and it's the reason a lot of portfolios are trailing the market by a wide margin without their owners realizing it.

Today's edition is a plain explanation of why the breakeven trap is so costly, why it feels so natural to do anyway, and how to actually break the habit.

Let's get into it.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

Why Waiting to Break Even Is So Expensive

A losing position has a steeper hill to climb than most investors realize. A stock that drops 50% doesn't need a 50% gain to break even. It needs a 100% gain.

That math gets more painful the bigger the loss. A 70% drop needs a 233% gain just to get back to where you started. A 90% drop needs a 900% gain. The percentages aren't symmetric, and the deeper the hole, the longer the climb.

But that's only the first half of the cost. The second half is what your money could have been doing somewhere else.

While you're waiting for a stuck stock to come back, every dollar tied up in that position is a dollar not earning a return anywhere else. The S&P 500 has historically returned somewhere around 10% per year on average. Each year you sit on a loser hoping for breakeven, the rest of the market is moving on without you.

A stock down 40% that takes five years to recover might feel like you "didn't lose anything" when you finally sell at the original price. But you actually missed five years of returns on that capital. The "breakeven" was an illusion.

The Anchor That Doesn't Actually Exist

The key here is that the price you paid for a stock has no bearing on what it's going to do next.

The market doesn't know what you paid, an it doesn't care. For better or worse, future returns are decided by what happens to the business from here, not by your personal history with the stock. Which is a major bummer, but that’s the way it goes… and many people (us included) struggle with that mentally.

Behavioral economists call this "anchoring." Your purchase price becomes a mental landmark that shapes your decisions, even though it's totally invisible to everyone else trading the same stock.

But the way out is to ignore your purchase price entirely. Just look at the stock fresh. Pretend you've never owned it. Then ask the real question: “Should I stay or should I go (now)?” And although it might feel like “If I go, there will be trouble, and if I stay, it will be double,” there is probably an answer (below).

The Better Question to Ask

Instead of "will this stock come back to what I paid or should I sell it?" try this one: "Would I buy this stock today, at the current price, with cash I had sitting around?"

That question trumps almost all of the breakeven thinking.

  • If the answer is yes, you've got real conviction. Hold the position.

  • If the answer is no, you don't actually like the stock at today's price… then there’s no reason to hold it anymore!

Most stuck positions fail this test. Investors hold losing stocks not because they believe in the company, but because selling would force them to admit a mistake. Holding to avoid that feeling isn't a strategy. It's an emotional default.

When Holding Is Actually the Right Call

That being said, not every losing position is a breakeven trap. Sometimes patience really is the right move.

The legitimate cases:

  • The story/thesis/narrative is genuinely intact. Earnings still growing, balance sheet still fine, the market is just temporarily down on the sector. This happens a lot during broader sell-offs.

  • You bought a quality business at a bad short-term price. It's not unusual for a great company to underperform for a few quarters while the longer story is still building.

  • You're holding for a specific catalyst that hasn't played out yet (a product launch, a regulatory decision, an earnings season you're waiting on).

The test isn't whether the position is currently down. The test is whether your reasons for owning it today still match the reasons you bought it in the first place.

How to Break the Habit

A few practical ways to get unstuck.

1. Tax-loss harvesting. If you're sitting on a loser in a taxable account, selling the position turns the paper loss into a tax deduction (it offsets gains, plus up to $3,000 of ordinary income a year). It's a way to make selling at a loss productive instead of painful. Just watch out for the wash-sale rule: if you rebuy the same stock within 30 days before or after the sale, the IRS disallows the loss.

2. Rotate into a similar but different name. If you still want exposure to the same theme, you can sell the underperformer and use the proceeds to buy a different, possibly stronger company in the same sector. You stay in the trade you wanted to be in. You just stop being attached to the specific stock that hasn't worked.

3. Pre-commit decision rules. Once a year, look at every position in your portfolio and ask the "would I buy this today?" question. Treat losers and winners with the same fresh eyes. Making it an annual routine takes most of the emotion out of the decision.

The breakeven trap is what happens when you treat your purchase price as a meaningful landmark, even though the market doesn't.

Three things to remember:

  • The math is asymmetric. A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to come back. The deeper the hole, the longer the climb.

  • The opportunity cost is real. Every dollar tied up in a stuck position is a dollar not earning a return somewhere else.

  • The right question isn't "will it come back?" The right question is "would I buy this today with fresh cash?"

The investors who do best over time aren't the ones who never make mistakes. They're the ones who let go of the mistakes quickly and put the money back to work.

Whatever Happened To…

For most of the 20th century, Jell-O was a powerhouse of American food culture, a product so embedded in everyday life that it seemed impossible it could ever fade. But fade it did, from the center of the dinner table to, well, mostly the hospital tray.

The story began in 1897, when a cough-syrup maker named Pearle Wait and his wife, May, mixed fruit flavoring into powdered gelatin in their kitchen in Le Roy, New York. May coined the name Jell-O, but the couple couldn't figure out how to sell it and unloaded the trademark for just $450. The buyer, a local businessman named Orator Woodward, launched a national advertising blitz, and by 1902, sales had hit $250,000.

The product took off during the Depression and World War II, when home cooks used Jell-O molds to stretch leftovers and dress up rationed ingredients. But the true golden age arrived in the 1950s. Gelatin molds became showpieces at dinner parties and potlucks, and women's magazines published elaborate recipes calling for everything from shredded cabbage to tuna to hard-boiled eggs suspended in shimmering lime or lemon gelatin.

Jell-O even released savory flavors, including celery, seasoned tomato, and Italian salad. On television, the brand's catchy jingle became one of the most recognizable in advertising.

It didn't last. By the mid-1970s,

savory Jell-O had fallen out of fashion . Americans were paying more attention to sugar intake, Julia Child was reviving interest in traditional cooking, and more women were entering the workforce with less time for elaborate molds.

The company pulled its savory varieties from shelves and pivoted hard toward marketing Jell-O as a treat for kids with Jell-O Pudding Pops, which lasted until the 1990s.

A brief relaunch under the Popsicle brand in 2004 fizzled. By then, Jell-O had settled into a quiet existence as a budget dessert and a staple of hospital clear-liquid diets, where it provided calories and hydration to patients who couldn't eat solid food.

Today, owner Kraft Heinz is trying to write a new chapter with last month's launch of Jell-O Simply, a line made with real fruit juice and 25% less sugar. And there's a small but growing retro food revival on social media, where younger cooks are sharing gelatin "food art" on TikTok and Instagram, treating the jiggly creations as equal parts nostalgia and spectacle.

It's a far cry from the shimmering centerpiece that once graced every church supper in America, but Jell-O is still wiggling.
