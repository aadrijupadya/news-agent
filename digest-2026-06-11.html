CPI Came In Hot at 4.2%, but the Core Number Brought Some Relief

The May inflation report tells two stories at once.

Headline CPI rose 0.5% in May and 4.2% over the past year, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. This is the hottest annual reading since April 2023, with energy doing most of the damage as the Iran conflict keeps a war premium baked into oil and gas.

But the part that matters more actually came in cooler. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy, rose just 0.2% on the month, below the 0.3% economists expected, leaving the annual core rate at 2.9%. That's the number the Fed watches most closely, and it came in softer than feared. In plain terms, the headline looks alarming, but it's almost entirely an energy story.

What it means for you

For the Fed, this looks like a "stay put" report, not a "hike rates now" one. A 4.2% headline driven by energy likely doesn’t force the Fed’s hand the way a hot core reading would have, so the path is probably rates on hold through the summer, with 2026 cuts unlikely (unless we get a huge economic slowdown).

Stocks are down on the inflation reading, but not brutally so. For your portfolio, the softer core inflation is why stocks don’t look worse than they do. Long-duration bonds and high-multiple tech are the most sensitive to inflation surprises, and a cooler core also probably takes some pressure off both. Energy still carries the war premium, which is why a small slice of it keeps acting as a natural hedge right now.

What to watch, and what to do

Watch wholesale inflation (PPI) Thursday and the Fed's June 16-17 meeting. High PPI would mean the energy spike is increasing the price of everything else, too; a tame PPI would back up today's "it's mostly just energy" read.

If you've been tempted to bail on bonds, the softer core inflation is a reason to breathe, not to panic. If you own zero energy, a small position earns its keep here as insurance. And if you're in cash, T-bill yields are still elevated because the Fed isn't cutting, so you're being paid to wait. The worst move today might be an emotional one off a scary headline.

World First: Patient Receives High-Risk Therapy to Make Cells Young Again

US-based biotechnology company Life Biosciences has dosed its first patients with an experimental therapy designed to reverse age-related vision loss. The treatment aims to turn back aging by activating three genes in nerves that do not naturally regenerate. The hope is that a single injection of the gene therapy, along with several weeks of antibiotics, can preserve or even restore vision. The genes are controlled by a genetic switch that only turns on when patients take the antibiotic, allowing for some level of control. The study will follow participants for at least five years.

The device sits on top of the brain membrane to decode brain signals in real-time, enabling patients to control a pneumatic glove with their thoughts. US-based Neuralink has so far implanted its N1 device in at least 21 patients under research protocols, but the company does not have commercial approval. Oracle Has the Biggest Book in Tech. The Bill Is Why the Stock Fell.

Oracle reported record fourth-quarter and full-year results after the bell on Wednesday: quarterly revenue of $19.2 billion (up 21%) and a record $67.4 billion for the year. But the number everyone fixated on was the backlog.

Oracle's remaining performance obligations, the value of signed contracts it hasn't delivered yet, jumped to $638 billion, up $85 billion in a single quarter and up 363% from a year ago. That's future revenue Oracle has booked but not yet earned, and it added more of it in 90 days than it makes in an entire year.

So why did a record quarter send the stock down more than 11%? In a word, financing. Oracle said it expects to raise about $40 billion in fiscal 2027 through debt and equity, including a $20 billion stock sale, on top of $43 billion in debt raised last year, with capital spending running near $55 billion. The market did the math: Oracle is borrowing heavily and diluting shareholders to fund a buildout whose payoff depends on a handful of AI customers, OpenAI chief among them, following through.

What it means for you

Oracle has become one of the purest bets on the AI-infrastructure buildout, and yesterday showed both sides of it. The demand is real and enormous, but fulfilling it means taking on debt, issuing stock, and leaning on customers who are themselves spending far more than they earn. Thus, the 11% drop in ORCL.

The AI buildout is increasingly financed in a circle:

  1. Chipmakers invest in model labs

  2. Labs sign huge cloud contracts

  3. Clouds borrow to buy more chips

  4. Repeat.

The market's message yesterday was that it’s seriously questioning how the AI dream gets financed, not just how big it is.
