Hello.

There's a moment that happens to almost every dividend investor at some point. You're scanning a list of high-yielding stocks looking for income, and one name jumps out: 8%, 9%, sometimes 10%+ yield. Way higher than anything else in the sector. Way higher than what your existing dividend holdings pay.

It looks like free money.

Sometimes it is. Sometimes it's a warning that the market has already priced in a dividend cut, and you're about to be the last person to find out.

Today's edition is a plain walk through how this happens, why an unusually high yield is often the market shouting at you, and how to actually tell whether a payout is sustainable or about to get slashed.

Let's get into it.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

Why a Big Yield Can Be a Warning Sign

Dividend yield is just the company's annual dividend divided by its stock price. So if a stock pays $4 a year in dividends and trades at $100, the yield is 4%.

Here's the part most people miss. Yield can rise for two completely different reasons. Either the dividend got bigger (good) or the stock price fell (often bad).

When the price drops, the yield goes up mechanically, even if the dividend hasn't changed. So a stock that was paying a 4% yield at $100 will be paying an 8% yield at $50, with no change at all to the actual dividend payment.

That's when the math starts shouting. If a stock's yield is suddenly twice what its peers pay, the market is usually telling you something. The most common message: investors are dumping the stock because they think the dividend is about to get cut.

When the Market Is Already Voting "Cut"

The market is forward-looking. Big institutional investors will often start selling a stock months before the company actually announces a dividend cut. They're reading the same financial statements you can read, just with full-time teams doing it.

By the time the company itself announces the cut, the stock price has often already dropped a lot. The "high yield" you saw on your screener was the price drop showing up in real time.

A few famous examples of yield traps that caught individual investors over the years:

  • General Electric was a household-name dividend stock for decades before its industrial business unraveled. The dividend got cut from 24 cents to 12 cents in 2017, then again to just one penny in 2018.

  • AT&T paid a 7%+ yield for years before spinning off its media business and cutting the annual dividend from $2.08 to $1.11 per share in 2022 (about a 47% reduction).

  • Wells Fargo got hit during the 2020 COVID downturn, cutting its quarterly dividend by roughly 80% (from 51 cents to 10 cents). Shell and BP also cut their dividends in 2020 (Shell's was its first dividend cut since World War II), though some other big energy names like ExxonMobil and Chevron held theirs steady.

Each of these names had a "free money" yield right before the cut. Each one was a value trap in real time.

How to Tell a Sustainable Payout from a Trap

Here are the things that actually matter when you're staring at a high-yielding stock and trying to decide whether to trust it.

1. The payout ratio. This is the share of the company's earnings that gets paid out as dividends. A payout ratio under about 60-70% usually leaves room for the dividend to keep growing. A payout ratio over 100% means the company is paying out more than it earns, which is only sustainable for so long.

2. Free cash flow coverage. This is the same idea but using actual cash instead of accounting earnings. Earnings can be smoothed by accountants. Cash either showed up or it didn't. If a company isn't generating enough free cash flow to cover its dividend, that's a flashing red light.

3. Earnings trend. Is the company growing, holding steady, or shrinking? A dividend that's only sustainable because the company isn't growing anymore is on a clock.

4. Debt load. Companies with a lot of debt sometimes cut the dividend to free up cash for debt payments. High dividend plus high debt equals more risk than the yield by itself suggests.

5. Dividend history. Has the company been consistently raising the dividend for years? Or has growth slowed to a crawl? A multi-year freeze in dividend raises is often a sign that management lacks confidence in the payout, and the next move could be a cut rather than a hike.

Red Flags vs. Green Flags

A quick way to scan any high-yielding stock you're considering.

Red flags:

  • Yield is dramatically higher than the sector average (twice or more).

  • Payout ratio is above 100%.

  • Earnings are declining year over year.

  • The most recent dividend was held flat instead of raised.

  • Management has started using language like "reviewing capital allocation" or "preserving flexibility" (analysts often read this as a soft signal that a cut is being considered).

Green flags:

  • Yield is in line with or modestly above peers.

  • Payout ratio is comfortably below 70% (or in line with industry norms).

  • Earnings and free cash flow are growing.

  • The company has raised its dividend every year for a decade or more.

  • Debt is manageable relative to the size of the business.

If a stock has three or more red flags, the yield is probably high for a reason. If it has three or more green flags, the payout is probably real and the high yield might actually be a buying opportunity.

🫡 See You Soon

A really high dividend yield can be one of two very different things:

  • The market overreacting to a temporary problem, leaving a real bargain on the table.

  • The market correctly anticipating a dividend cut, and putting you on the wrong side of the trade.

Telling them apart is mostly about checking a handful of simple things:

  • Is the payout ratio reasonable for the industry?

  • Is free cash flow covering the dividend?

  • Are earnings growing or shrinking?

  • How much debt does the company carry?

  • Has the dividend actually been growing?

If most of those answers are "yes" or "fine," you might be looking at a real opportunity. If most of them are "no" or "worried," the high yield is the warning sign, not the prize.

Whatever Happened To…

Highlights magazine?

Picture every doctor's office of your childhood. The vinyl chairs. The fish tank, maybe. And on the table,

a copy ofHighlights for Children

. For millions of American kids, Highlights was the first magazine they ever read, usually while waiting for a shot or the dentist’s drill.

For children, it was an escape. They hunted for the hidden pictures (a toothbrush tucked into a tree branch, a slice of pie hiding in a cloud). And everyone checked in on

Goofus and Gallant , the two boys who faced the same situation on every page: the wild Goofus grabbing the last cookie, the polite Gallant offering it to a friend.

Husband-and-wife educators Garry Cleveland Myers and Caroline Clark Myers launched the magazine in June 1946 from Honesdale, Pennsylvania, and when it nearly folded a few years later, their son quit his job as an aeronautical engineer to rescue it. His big idea? Mail copies to doctors' and dentists' offices.

Circulation hit one million in 1971, peaked near three million in the 1980s, and in 2006 the Postal Service delivered the one-billionth copy—all without ever printing a single ad.

Whatever Happened To…

Highlights now publishes six magazines with a combined circulation of about a million a month, including spinoffs for babies, preschoolers, and puzzle-loving tweens.

And the company isn't just hanging on; it's growing. This April,

Highlightsand Google announced that their joint online-safety classroom program had reached one million students.

A 2018 documentary about the magazine,

44 Pages, is

streaming on Prime Video . But you could still just look around next time you're stuck in a pediatrician’s waiting room. The odds are decent that a copy of Highlights is sitting on the table nearby.

The dirty little secret behind “hot” IPOs

By its very nature, an IPO seems, well, public.

There’s the filing, the interviews, the hush before the bell, and finally…

The opening trade.

That’s when the public conversation officially begins.

What no one ever hears about are the hush-hush transactions that happened earlier.

Before many companies approach public markets, early employees and venture investors sometimes sell portions of their shares in private secondary transactions.

For example, an employee who has held stock for years may want liquidity, or a venture fund may want to return capital.

Instead of waiting for the IPO, those shares are sold privately to institutional buyers at negotiated prices…

And those deals leave clues long before a ticker symbol exists.

Hello.

Every quarter, nearly every public company you own holds an earnings call. It's basically an hour-long conversation between the company's top executives and the Wall Street analysts who cover the stock, and it's where most of the real information about the business actually comes out.

Here's the thing: nobody actually has an hour. So everyone (the funds, the analysts, the journalists writing the next morning's headlines) is really just looking at the same handful of moments inside the call.

Today's edition is your 10-minute version of what to look for. What actually moves a stock on earnings, how to read between the lines of management's guidance, and the part of the call that's almost always more revealing than the prepared statement.

Let's get into it.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

The Headline Beat Isn't the Story

The first thing most people read after earnings is whether the company "beat" or "missed" expectations on revenue and earnings. Every financial article leads with that number.

But stocks can fall on beats and rally on misses. The reason is that the market already priced in the expected number weeks before the report came out. By the time the quarter is announced, what happened in the past three months is old news. What matters is what management says about the future.

A recent example: CrowdStrike beat the quarter on revenue and earnings, raised its full-year outlook, and even announced a 4-for-1 stock split. The stock fell anyway. The reason wasn't the past quarter at all. It was that one forward-looking number (billings, which signals next quarter's revenue for a subscription business) came in below expectations.

What Actually Moves the Stock: The Guidance

Most earnings calls include some version of forward guidance. Sometimes, it is specific ranges for next quarter's revenue and earnings. Other times, it's an updated full-year outlook. Sometimes it's a multi-year target.

This is the part the market actually trades on. Whether management raises, holds, or lowers their guidance will usually drive the stock's reaction more than the quarter that just ended.

Three patterns to know:

  • Raise: management increases prior estimates for the year. Usually good for the stock, though not bulletproof. If another forward-looking number (like billings) disappoints in the same report, even a raise can get overshadowed.

  • Hold: management reaffirms prior estimates. Neutral, but the reaction depends on what the market was expecting.

  • Lower or withdraw: management cuts guidance or pulls it altogether. Almost always bad outside of broad market shocks, and often worse than the headline suggests because it signals real uncertainty.

Pay close attention to the language. "We continue to expect" is different from "we now expect." The first means nothing changed. The second means something changed, and you want to know what.

Decoding the Guidance Language

A lot of corporate guidance language has a real translation underneath. A starter dictionary:

  • Modest growth: "we'd be embarrassed by zero growth, but don't expect us to surprise you to the upside."

  • Cautious near-term: "the next quarter might be ugly, but the longer story is intact."

  • Lapping a tough comp: "last year's number was unusually strong, so this year will look smaller by comparison." (Often defensive, not necessarily bad.)

  • Macroeconomic headwinds: "the broader economy is a problem, and that's our explanation for whatever you're about to hear."

  • Strategic investments: "we're spending money that will hurt the bottom line in the short run but we believe will pay off."

The rule of thumb: when management hedges aggressively, the stock usually drops. When they sound confident, the stock usually holds or rises. The market reads tone like a kid reads a parent's face.

The Q&A Is Where the Truth Comes Out

The first half of an earnings call is the prepared remarks. The CEO and CFO read carefully written statements that say roughly the same thing as the press release.

The second half is the Q&A with Wall Street analysts. This is where you should actually pay attention.

Analysts spend their entire careers covering a small number of companies. They've been listening to these calls for years. They know exactly which questions the company doesn't want to answer, and they ask them anyway.

Three things to watch for in the Q&A:

  • A question that gets dodged. Management gives a vague answer and quickly moves on. That's the question worth knowing the real answer to.

  • An analyst pushing back. The analyst doesn't accept the first answer and re-asks. That means the original answer didn't satisfy a person who's paid to be skeptical of the company.

  • A question about a specific number management didn't mention in their prepared remarks. If something material was missing from the company's own summary, the analysts will find it.

This is the section of the call where the stock often moves in real time. If you can only listen to one part, this is the part.

The 10-Minute Checklist

If you only have ten minutes, here's what to actually look at:

  1. Read the press release. Headline revenue and earnings, beat or miss versus expectations.

  2. Find the guidance section. Did the company raise, hold, or lower their outlook?

  3. Read management's own language about the next quarter. Confident? Cautious? Defensive?

  4. Skim the first three analyst questions. Those are usually about the biggest issues.

  5. Look for any question that got a vague answer or a re-ask.

That's basically the whole thing. You don't need to read the full transcript. You don't need to listen to the whole call. The handful of moments above will tell you the bulk of what actually moved the stock.

For finding transcripts: Seeking Alpha, Motley Fool, and the company's own investor relations page typically post earnings call transcripts the same day or the next morning. Most are free.

The headline beat or miss is the smallest part of an earnings call. What actually moves a stock is:

  • The forward guidance, especially whether it was raised, held, or lowered.

  • The language management uses about the next quarter and year (confident or defensive).

  • The Q&A, where analysts ask the questions management would rather skip.

Spend ten minutes on those three things and you'll know more about the company's near-term trajectory than most of the people writing about it the next morning.
