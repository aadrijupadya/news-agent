SpaceX's IPO Dream Runs Into Wall Street's Oldest Test

SpaceX priced its IPO at $135 per share on Thursday, raising $75 billion in what's expected to be the biggest public offering in history when shares begin trading Friday. The company has rockets, satellites, Starlink, defense demand, and one of the biggest private-market stories on the planet. What it may not have is the one thing Wall Street eventually asks every growth story to show: that the business can make money. Also, side note, Elon’s mom is at the Nasdaq.

SpaceX's filing shows the scale, but also the gap. The company generated nearly $19 billion in revenue in 2025 but posted a net loss of nearly $5 billion. That gives investors plenty of business to analyze, but not a profitable one yet.

A great company can still be a rough trade on IPO day. After the opening rush, the question shifts from who wants access to what the business can already prove. That's where the data gets uncomfortable.

What it means for you

According to IPO data from the University of Florida's Jay Ritter, money-losing companies had the louder opening act, jumping 26.5% on average on their first day. But three years later, their average return was slightly negative. Profitable IPOs did the opposite: fewer first-day fireworks, better staying power.

SpaceX isn't a tiny startup, and that matters. IPOs with more than $100 million in pre-debut revenue had smaller opening pops but much stronger returns over the next three years than companies below that line. Tech also helps; tech IPOs beat non-tech IPOs after the first close, but the category still needs a real business underneath.

The takeaway is simple: Losses make the IPO pop louder. Sales and tech help. Profits are what matter long term after the confetti clears, either way.

EvoArena: Tracking Memory Evolution for Robust LLM Agents in Dynamic Environments
This paper proposes EvoArena, a framework that tracks how an LLM agent's memory evolves over time to improve robustness when operating in dynamic environments where conditions change. The work is significant because it identifies that standard LLM agents fail catastrophically when deployed environments shift, and solving this via memory evolution directly addresses a critical real-world deployment challenge.
MaxProof: Scaling Mathematical Proof with Generative-Verifier RL and Population-Level Test-Time Scaling
MaxProof combines reinforcement learning with a verifier model and population-level test-time scaling to improve LLMs' ability to solve mathematical proofs at scale. This is novel because it demonstrates that integrating verification feedback into the generation process plus sampling multiple solutions significantly outperforms standard approaches, pushing LLMs toward genuine mathematical reasoning.
MiniMax Sparse Attention
MiniMax Sparse Attention proposes a structured sparsity pattern for transformer attention that reduces computational complexity while maintaining model quality. This matters because attention is a computational bottleneck in LLMs, and a practical sparse attention mechanism could enable faster inference and longer context windows without retraining from scratch.
WeaveBench: A Long-Horizon, Real-World Benchmark for Computer-Use Agents with Hybrid Interfaces
WeaveBench introduces the first comprehensive benchmark for evaluating agents that interact with realistic web and desktop environments combining both HTML and visual interfaces over long task horizons. This is important because existing benchmarks focus on single interface types, but real computer-use agents must handle mixed interfaces, making WeaveBench a critical evaluation tool for autonomous computer interaction.
