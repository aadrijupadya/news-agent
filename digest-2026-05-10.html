Stop Investing Like Warren Buffett
html

Hello.

Today we're pressure-testing a piece of conventional wisdom that's been making the rounds in investor group chats: "If Warren Buffett is sitting on $390 billion in cash, he must know something. Maybe I should wait too."

It's a tempting frame. Buffett built his reputation on patience and timing. If the greatest investor of his generation is parked in T-bills, the rational move for a retail investor is to do the same… at least until prices drop, the war ends, the Fed cuts, the election clears, or whatever the next reason is.

We think that's wrong. Not because Buffett is wrong about valuations; he might be exactly right. We think it's wrong because Buffett's situation looks nothing like yours. Confusing the two is one of the more expensive mistakes a retail investor can make.

Here's why.

1. He's playing a different game

Berkshire Hathaway is a $1+ trillion permanent-capital vehicle. When Buffett buys, he doesn't pick up 100 shares of Apple. He buys $40 billion worth… or the entire company. As we said, different game. 

Most public assets aren't big enough to move his needle. He's not waiting for good opportunities. He's waiting for elephant-sized ones.

You don't have that problem. There are reasonable opportunities at every market cap, sector, and yield, most of which are completely irrelevant to Buffett because he can't size up a position big enough to matter.

When Buffett says "nothing looks attractive," what he often means is "nothing looks attractive at the size I need it to be." That's a very different sentence!

2. He's getting paid a fortune to wait

Cash isn't a free option. For most retail investors, the opportunity cost is whatever the market does over the same period. For Buffett, it's also that, but with one enormous asterisk.

Berkshire's $390 billion is parked almost entirely in short-term Treasuries yielding around 4-5%. That's roughly $16-19 billion a year in risk-free interest income, just sitting in cash.

Read that again. 

While Buffett "waits," he's earning more in annual T-bill interest than the entire annual revenue of most S&P 500 companies. His cash is a productive position, not idle dry powder.

You can buy short Treasuries too. SGOV, BIL, and money markets all yield in that range. But the math doesn't scale the way it does for him. A $50,000 portfolio earning 4.5% on cash makes $2,250 a year. That's basically just a credit card cash-back equivalent. 

3. His mandate is permanent. Yours isn't.

Buffett is 95 years old, runs a public holding company, and isn't trying to retire on his portfolio. He has no benchmark, no withdrawal schedule, no liability matching. He can wait five more years for a setup he likes. He'll be fine either way.

Most retail investors are running a much harder problem: a finite working life, a retirement target, and a portfolio that has to outpace inflation over decades. Sitting in cash isn't free for you. Every year you're not invested is a year of compounding you don't get back.

So why do people freeze?

Because the standard advice ("buy the index, lump sum, today") runs face-first into the human brain.

Vanguard's research is clear on the math. Lump-sum investing beats dollar-cost averaging about two-thirds of the time, because markets go up most of the time and being invested earlier means more compounding. On paper, it's the right answer.

In practice? Almost nobody does it. Hand someone $100,000 the morning the Nasdaq's at all-time highs. But with a war in the Middle East, the Fed uncertain, and Buffett in cash, most investors freeze. They wait a week. Then a month. Then six months. The "lump sum" decision quietly becomes "no decision," which is itself a decision to stay in cash.

This is where DCA earns its keep. Not as the optimal strategy, but as the executable one.

The DCA workaround

Dollar-cost averaging is the investing of a fixed amount on a regular schedule regardless of price. NOTE: It’s mathematically inferior to lump sum about 67% of the time. We're not pretending otherwise.

But it’s the strategy you're more likely to actually stick with, and so it’s the one that works!

The practical framework:

  • If you're 100% sure you can lump sum and not check your portfolio for years, then lump sum. The math is on your side.

  • If you're going to second-guess yourself or panic-sell on the first 5% drawdown, split your buy into 6-12 monthly tranches. You'll likely give up some return, but you'll also actually be invested.

  • If you're doing it from a paycheck (401k, IRA contributions), you're already DCA'ing whether you call it that or not. Keep going.

The trick isn't picking the optimal entry. The trick is being in the market. It’s a tired adage for a reason: time in the market beats timing the market. That includes timing it against Buffett's cash pile.

Bottom Line

Buffett has $390 billion in cash because he runs a $1 trillion permanent-capital vehicle that has to deploy in elephant-sized chunks, while earning $16-19 billion a year in T-bill interest, with no retirement deadline and no benchmark. That is not your situation.

The mistake isn't disagreeing with Buffett. The mistake is using his portfolio decisions as a substitute for making your own.

If you can't bring yourself to lump sum at all-time highs in the middle of a war (spoiler alert: most people can't), then DCA into the market on a schedule. Set the auto-buy. Stop checking the news. Stop waiting for permission from a 95-year-old in Omaha. (To be clear, we love Buffett. We talk about him all the time and send you his quotes every week!)

The best portfolio strategy is the one you can actually execute.

Whatever Happened To…

Father Edward Flanagan built Boys Town on one of the most radical ideas in early 20th century America: that there was “no such thing as a bad boy.”

In 1917, the Irish-born Catholic priest opened a small home for homeless and delinquent boys in Omaha, at a time when troubled children were often dumped into prisons or brutal reform schools. What began as a modest shelter soon grew into a self-governing village where boys elected mayors, judges, and commissioners, an experiment in citizenship wrapped in Christian charity.

Here’s Father Flanagan talking about its origins .

The timing was perfect. During the Depression, Americans were desperate for stories of redemption and hope. Boys Town offered both. Then Hollywood arrived. The 1938 film Boys Town , starring Spencer Tracy as Flanagan and Mickey Rooney as a hardened juvenile delinquent, turned the priest into a national folk hero.

You can watch the trailer here .

Tracy won an Oscar, donations poured in, and Boys Town became one of the most admired charities in the country. For mid-century America, it symbolized the belief that compassion, discipline, and community could rescue almost any child.

But Boys Town also belonged to a particular vision of America. It belonged to an America that believed moral authority could reform society, institutions could shape character, priests and civic leaders deserved trust, and national culture could unite around common myths. That America began to vanish after the 1960s.

The country grew more skeptical of churches, paternalism, and grand moral crusades. Experts shifted toward foster care and smaller community programs instead of giant residential campuses. Fundraising controversies in the 1970s further tarnished Boys Town’s saintly image.

The organization survived by reinventing itself as a broad nonprofit network focused on behavioral health, counseling, and family services.

Boys Town still exists today

. What disappeared was the myth.
