International Is Actually Leading the S&P 500 Right Now

Vanguard's total-international ETF (VXUS) is up 10.3% year-to-date at the time of writing. The S&P 500 is behind by more than a whole percentage point. 

For most US-based investors, international has been the part of the portfolio you ignore. That assumption is starting to look out of date. The bigger question now is which markets are doing the actual work.

The Strength Is Concentrated in Three Stories

Japan: corporate governance just got a real shove. The Tokyo Stock Exchange began pressuring listed companies to focus on capital efficiency a number of years ago. Firms trading below book value were specifically asked to publish plans to fix it.

The result: Japanese share buybacks set new records for five straight years and hit 22.32 trillion Yen (roughly $142 billion) in fiscal 2025.

Berkshire Hathaway noticed. Berkshire now owns more than 10% of each of Japan's five major trading houses (Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo, Itochu, Marubeni).

South Korea: the AI memory cycle. One of the best-performing major international ETFs in 2026 is iShares MSCI South Korea (EWY), up 78% YTD and driven largely by Samsung and SK Hynix. Both are critical suppliers of the high-bandwidth memory chips that AI training clusters depend on.

The AI infrastructure trade has been a US-tech story for years. But it’s also a Korean story too.

Brazil: commodity export surge. Favorable terms of trade paired with global demand for natural resources have fueled massive profits for state-owned giants like Petrobras (PBR) and iron ore leader Vale (VALE). As the central bank moves into a major interest rate easing cycle, global capital is flowing back into these heavily undervalued equities.

Where the Bull Case Hasn't Worked

India is the conspicuous disappointment. iShares MSCI India (INDA) is down more than 11% year-to-date in May 2026 after years of being the consensus emerging-market growth story.

The structural case (demographics, GDP growth, China+1 supply-chain rotation) is intact. The problem was valuation. Indian stocks had run to historically high price-to-earnings ratios at their 2024 peak, and investors finally rotated to cheaper markets. This is a valuation reset, not a fundamental break.

Indonesia is in a severe policy crisis. iShares MSCI Indonesia (EIDO) is down nearly 30% year-to-date in May 2026. 

The structural drivers of the prior bull case (the nickel commodity boom, downstream industrialization, expanding consumer tech) are being heavily undermined by sudden state intervention.

Most mainstream guidance (Vanguard, Morningstar) suggests an international allocation somewhere between 20% and 40% of the equity portion of a portfolio.

If you currently hold zero international (which describes a lot of US-focused retail portfolios), the case for adding some exposure now is stronger than it has been in years. The valuation gap is meaningful. The dollar is weakening. Specific stories (Japan, Korea, Brazil) have had real catalysts.

International stocks are outperforming the US. The strength isn't broad. It's concentrated in a few places.

The long-term picture still favors the US. The shorter-term picture seems to reward diversification in our opinion. If your portfolio currently runs zero international exposure, this is a reasonable moment to revisit that decision.

Most Retail Investors Buy Single Stocks for the Wrong Reasons

A simple framework  ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌

You read about a stock that sounds amazing. Maybe it's a chip company, a beaten-down retailer, a biotech with a promising drug. The question is whether you should buy that one stock, or buy the ETF that holds it alongside its competitors.

Most retail investors default to the single stock because it's more exciting. But that’s not always the best option.

As you know, single stocks can absolutely beat their sector ETFs by huge margins (Apple and Nvidia both did, by enormous multiples over the long run). But the challenge is figuring out when you're picking the next Nvidia and when you're picking the next dot-com bust.

Question 1: Can You Name Three Things This Company Does Better Than Its Competitors?

Knowing exactly why a company is winning is the single biggest separator between an investor who picks great single stocks and one who gets burned by hot tips. If you can name three real advantages, you have a reason to own the stock. If you can't, default to the ETF.

How this works: Great long-term winners almost always have what investors call "moats." These are structural reasons the company can keep beating its competition for years. Things like:

  • Brand power and patents that customers and competitors can't easily replicate

  • Network effects, where the product gets more valuable as more people use it (think Visa, Uber, or Meta)

  • Switching costs that make leaving painful (think your iPhone ecosystem, or the enterprise software your company runs on)

  • Cost advantages from scale that smaller competitors can't match (think Costco or Amazon)

If you can name three real moats off the top of your head, you have a thesis. And "they're a hot stock" doesn't count!

The catch is that it's easy to confuse a product with an advantage. "Apple makes iPhones" is a product. "Apple's ecosystem makes it expensive for consumers to leave it because all their photos, contacts, and apps live within it" is a moat. Be specific or you don't pass.

What to do:

  • Spend ten minutes writing down three real competitive advantages for the company you're thinking about. Be honest about whether they're moats or just marketing.

  • If you can't get to three, consider buying the sector ETF instead. You'll still get exposure to the trend, without taking the risk of betting big on a company you don't fully understand.

Question 2: Would You Hold This Stock Through a 30% Drop?

A stock can be a long-term winner and still lose 30% (or 50%, or 80%) along the way. If you'd hold through that, you have the temperament for single-stock investing. If you'd sell at the bottom, save yourself the pain and buy the ETF.

Why this works: Every great stock you've heard of has lost at least 30% of its value at some point, often multiple times.

  • Apple lost more than 80% during the dot-com bust before its iPhone-era comeback.

  • Nvidia has had multiple 50%+ drops, including a roughly 67% drawdown in 2022 alone.

The investors who got rich on those names are the ones who didn't sell during the drops. The ones who panic-sold near the lows ended up funding the recovery for everyone else.

The issue here is that it's easy to say "I'd hold" when the stock is up and you're feeling smart. The actual test is what you'd do at 3 a.m. after a brutal earnings report, when you've lost a third of your money on paper and the financial press is asking whether the company is "broken." Most people overestimate their stomach for that moment.

What to do:

  • Imagine the dollar amount, not the percentage. If you put $5,000 into the stock and it dropped to $3,500 in three weeks, would you genuinely hold? Or would you sell to "preserve what's left"?

  • If you'd sell, the ETF is probably the smarter play. You'll get most of the upside with a fraction of the gut-check moments, and you won't have to make a high-stakes decision at the worst possible time.

What Happens If You Pass Both Tests?

If you can name three real advantages AND you'd genuinely hold through a 30% drop, the single stock has earned its place in your portfolio.

The reason this matters: ETFs spread your money across dozens of names, which protects you when one company blows up but also means you only get a small slice of any single winner.

If a stock returned 200x over a decade (the Nvidia 10-year story), an ETF holding it alongside dozens of competitors captured only a fraction of that gain. Both crushed the S&P 500, but the gap between them is the difference between owning the winner directly and owning the winner inside a basket with other assets.

That gap only matters if you want to actually try and pick a winner. If you don’t want to do that (and no shame if you don’t), the ETF might be the better deal.

The point of investing over long time horizons is that you’re able to ride out drawdowns and still grow over time. Put another way: if your portfolio is so “safe” that you never experience drawdowns… you probably won’t experience much growth, either.

A Real-World Worked Example

Say you've been hearing about Nvidia and want to decide. Run the framework:

Three competitive advantages?

  1. CUDA, Nvidia's software platform, is the de facto standard software stack for AI compute. Competing hardware exists, but the AI development tools (PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX) are all built to run on CUDA first. (Switching cost.)

  2. Nvidia's manufacturing relationship with TSMC gives them first access to the most advanced chip-making technology. (Cost and scale advantage.)

  3. The biggest cloud and AI customers have stated multi-year demand visibility for Nvidia GPUs. (Customer lock-in; this includes major hyperscalers like Microsoft, Amazon, Google, and Meta.)

Would you hold through a 30% drop? Nvidia’s stock price was cut in half in 2022 and recovered to new all-time highs within two years. If you'd panicked, you missed the recovery. Be honest about whether you would have actually held.

If you can confidently answer yes to both, owning Nvidia directly might make sense. If you can't, you can still get plenty of upside from the broader semiconductor ETFs. The two main ones are SMH (VanEck) and SOXX (iShares). Either gets you Nvidia plus its peers, with much less single-company risk.

Wrapping up

The framework, one more time:

  1. Can you name three real competitive advantages? If no, buy the ETF.

  2. Would you hold through a 30% drop? If no, buy the ETF.

  3. If yes to both, the single stock has earned its place in your portfolio.

Most readers won't pass both tests for most stocks they're interested in, and that's the right answer. Pick the single stock when you actually have a thesis and the stomach to back it up. Pick the ETF when you don't. Either way you're in the market, which is most of what matters.

Whatever Happened To…

For a brief stretch in the late 1960s and 1970s, the fondue pot was the center of American social life. If you hosted a dinner party, chances were good that a small flame was heating melted cheese in the middle of your table.

Fondue arrived in the United States through postwar travel and savvy Swiss promotion. American tourists encountered it in Alpine resorts, while Swiss cheese producers pushed the dish abroad to increase exports.

The

1964 New York World’s Fair helped introduce fondue to a mass audience, and by the end of the decade it was showing up in cookbooks, department stores, and suburban kitchens.

The concept fit perfectly with the era’s dinner-party culture. Young couples entertaining at home liked foods that were interactive and informal. Fondue turned dinner into a group activity. Guests gathered around a pot of melted cheese, dipping cubes of bread with long forks.

Variations followed. Some hosts used hot oil to cook meat at the table, while chocolate fondue became a popular dessert.

Buying a fondue set became almost a rite of passage for new households.

Restaurants joined the trend as well (

see this video ), and a few managed to outlast the fad. Despite the collapse of the home fondue party craze, the concept survived in a narrower niche. The biggest example is The Melting Pot, founded in Florida in 1975 and now operating roughly 90 locations across the United States and Canada.

Today fondue occupies a strange place in American food culture. It is mostly nostalgia. Many people associate it with 1970s entertaining or romantic restaurants. Yet the idea never fully disappeared.
