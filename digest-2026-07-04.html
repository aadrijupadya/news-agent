💾 The Memory Trade: How the Most Boring Chip in Tech Took Over the Market

For decades, memory chips were the definition of a brutal, boring business. Interchangeable, endlessly cyclical, priced like a commodity. When demand rose, everyone built more, prices crashed, and the whole industry spent a year losing money.

But that script just got torn up.

On June 24, Micron reported one of the most staggering quarters we have ever seen from a chipmaker, and it put hard numbers on something the market has been circling for months. Traders call it “the memory trade."

(Don’t worry, you don’t need to be technical to understand this; we’re breaking it all down simple below.)

Today we’re breaking down what the memory trade actually is, why it suddenly matters to almost every portfolio, why Micron's earnings set off such a reaction, and where it could go from here.

Here's the tour:

🧠 What the "memory trade" actually is
⚡ Why it suddenly matters (AI changed the math)
📊 Why everyone is talking about Micron's June 24 earnings
👀 Where things could go from here

Let’s begin.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

What the "Memory Trade" Actually Is

There are two big families of memory chips. DRAM is a computer's short-term working memory. NAND flash is long-term storage. Both have historically been commodities, sold on price.

The new star is a specialized DRAM called “high-bandwidth memory” (HBM): many DRAM chips stacked together so they can feed an AI accelerator (think Nvidia's chips) fast enough to keep up. What you need to know is that every AI server needs a lot of it.

In fact, AI's appetite for HBM is so enormous that the three companies who dominate memory (Micron, plus South Korea's Samsung and SK Hynix) have pointed roughly 93% of their combined capacity at it. That turns a boring commodity into a scarce, high-margin product, and starves the ordinary memory market that everything else depends on (like smart phones, laptops, etc).

The economics explain the frenzy. An HBM stack sells for roughly $60 to $100, versus $5 to $10 for a comparable amount of normal memory. So the "memory trade" is the bet that this shift, from commodity to sold-out strategic input, keeps rewarding the makers like Micron.

Why It Suddenly Matters

For years, owning a memory stock meant timing a boom and hoping you sold before the crash. The pitch now is that AI has structurally changed demand, at least for a while.

Prices show it. Samsung reportedly raised a 32-gigabyte memory module to $239 from $149, a 60% jump, and SK Hynix said a while back that its capacity is essentially sold out for 2026.

This reaches your daily life. When memory gets scarce, everything that uses it costs more to build, which is why Apple has raised prices on MacBooks and iPads and others have followed. Some call it an "AI memory tax" on your next device.

It also ties into the biggest story in the market. Hyperscalers are locking up roughly half of all DRAM and NAND production through long-term deals. Memory prices are now a direct read on the AI infrastructure spending wave.

Why Everyone Is Talking About Micron's June 24 Earnings

Micron is the only one of the big three that trades as a US stock, so it's the cleanest way to play the trade. Its fiscal third-quarter results, from Micron's own release, were the loudest confirmation yet:

  • Revenue of about $41.5 billion, up from just $9.3 billion a year earlier

  • Gross margin near 85%, almost unheard of for a memory company

  • Net income around $29 billion, and earnings of $25.11 per share (up from $12.20)

  • Data-center revenue of about $25 billion, and record NAND revenue of $9.9 billion, up 361%

Management said HBM is fully booked through 2027 with demand into 2028, and that its newest HBM4 is already shipping for Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin platform. The company's summary was blunt: demand continues to outpace supply. The stock jumped about 14.6% after the print.

The reason it landed so hard: it moved the memory trade from thesis to receipt. For months the story was "AI needs memory and there isn't enough." Micron just showed what that looks like on an income statement.

Where Things Could Go From Here

There are two sides here:

The bulls point to how sold-out the industry is. Supply is spoken for into 2027 and into 2028, hyperscalers are signing long-term deals, and Micron has even stepped back from low-margin consumer memory to focus on AI. In that view, the makers finally have lasting pricing power.

The skeptics point to one word: cyclical. Memory is the most boom-and-bust corner of tech, and record margins are exactly what lure the industry into building more. New capacity is due in 2027 and 2028, and when supply has caught up before, these stocks have fallen hard and fast. The whole trade also depends on AI spending staying elevated, and the market is already nervous: the Magnificent Seven are down more than 13% from their May highs as investors question the roughly $725 billion Big Tech plans to spend on AI this year. There's even a DRAM price-fixing lawsuit against the three memory makers.

A few things are worth watching here: hyperscaler capital-spending guidance, HBM booking commentary, memory pricing trends, and any wave of new fab announcements.

In summary, the memory trade is a bet on AI infrastructure demand, sitting on top of the most cyclical business in tech. It can be a fine thing to own and a dangerous thing to chase. If it earns a spot in your portfolio, size it like the volatile position it is.

🫡 See You Soon

The takeaways:

  • Memory stopped being a commodity, at least for now. AI's hunger for high-bandwidth memory turned a boring chip into a scarce, high-margin product.

  • Micron's June 24 quarter was the proof. Record revenue, an 85% gross margin, and a backlog booked into 2028.

  • The upside and the risk are the same fact. This is a bet on AI spending staying strong, on top of the most cyclical business in tech. Own it that way, or not at all.

Nobody rings a bell at the top of a supercycle. Watch the demand signals, respect the cycle, and let the position be as big as your conviction, not your FOMO.

Rare 'Declaration' Copy Found in London

A volunteer at Britain's National Archives has discovered a

rare 1776 printing of the Declaration of Independence, one of only 11 surviving "Exeter Declarations" and the only copy known outside the United States.

The document was

seized on Christmas Eve 1776

when the Royal Navy captured the American privateer Dalton off Portugal after a seven-hour chase. Logged simply as "another paper," it sat unnoticed for 250 years.

"Exeter Declarations" are a specific batch of early printed copies of the Declaration of Independence, produced by printer Robert Luist Fowle in Exeter, New Hampshire, between July 16 and 19, 1776, roughly two weeks after the original was adopted in Philadelphia on July 4.

Volunteer Michael Scurr found it while cataloguing Navy correspondence. It will go on display in the Archives'

Revolution 250 exhibition , running through November.

Hello.

The June jobs report landed with a thud on Thursday, released a day early ahead of the holiday: just 57,000 payrolls added versus the roughly 115,000 economists expected.

A number that soft is a signal that growth is cooling. And when growth cools, money tends to drift toward the sectors that hold up no matter what the economy is doing.

That rotation has been building for weeks. Technology just came off a rough stretch (chipmakers sold off hard to start the month), while healthcare has stepped into sector leadership, with the Healthcare Select Sector SPDR (XLV) pressing up toward fresh 52-week highs.

This is neither panic nor overreaction. It is portfolio managers doing what they always do when growth softens: leaning toward earnings stability.

The Alternative: Municipal Bond CEFs

Here is an asset class that generates tax-free income and trades at a discount to its holdings.

Municipal bond closed-end funds buy portfolios of state and local government debt and distribute the interest income monthly. Because CEFs have a fixed number of shares trading on an exchange, their market price can diverge from the net asset value of their holdings.

According to VanEck's analysis, buying when discounts are wide (7%+ below NAV) has historically delivered +11% over the following year, positive 91% of the time. Current discounts sit in the 3-6% range after spring's volatility.

Names to research:

  • Nuveen Municipal Credit Income Fund (NZF): one of the larger muni CEFs

  • VanEck CEF Muni Income ETF (XMPT): an ETF holding a basket of muni CEFs

The opportunity: You are buying bonds at a discount, collecting tax-advantaged monthly income, and positioning for potential price appreciation if the discount narrows.

The risk: Leverage cuts both ways. If rates rise or municipal credit quality deteriorates, leveraged CEFs can decline faster than unleveraged alternatives.
