Eaton Corporation (ETN)

If Nvidia builds the brain, Eaton builds the nervous system that connects it to the power grid.

Eaton manufactures everything a data center needs that isn't a GPU: switchgear, power distribution units, uninterruptible power supplies, and now (thanks to a recent acquisition) liquid cooling systems. Every time a hyperscaler announces another $50 billion in AI infrastructure spending, a significant share flows directly to Eaton's order book.

  • The Numbers: First-quarter 2026 revenue hit a record $7.5 billion, up 17% year-over-year. Adjusted EPS came in at $2.81, and management raised full-year guidance to $13.05-$13.50, representing roughly 10% growth over 2025.

  • The Moat: Eaton's competitive position rests on being an end-to-end solutions provider in a market where customers don't want to piece together power infrastructure from five different vendors. The company just closed $11 billion in acquisitions, including Boyd Thermal, a liquid-cooling specialist serving hyperscalers.

  • The Opportunity: Data center orders surged 240% year-over-year in Q1. Total electrical backlog climbed 48%. Management disclosed that U.S. data center capacity under construction has grown to 32 gigawatts, with a total planned backlog of 165 to 228 gigawatts through 2030. That's 12 years of building at 2025 installation rates.

  • Dividend Yield: 1.1%

  • Biggest Risk: Valuation. At roughly 30x forward earnings, Eaton isn't cheap. A lot of growth is already priced in, and if AI infrastructure spending slows, the multiple compresses.

Caterpillar (CAT)

Caterpillar is a Dividend Aristocrat with 32 consecutive years of dividend growth. You can't build a data center without heavy equipment. You can't build the transmission lines connecting them to the grid without excavators. And you definitely can't expand mining operations for the copper and rare earths those data centers require without Caterpillar machines.

Caterpillar benefits when multiple secular trends converge: AI infrastructure buildout, reshoring, grid modernization, and commodity demand. The dealership network spanning 180 countries creates a recurring services revenue stream that competitors struggle to match.

  • The Numbers: Q1 2026 sales hit $17.4 billion, up 22% from the prior year. EPS of $5.54 beat analyst estimates by nearly 20%. The company deployed $5.7 billion in share repurchases and dividends in the quarter alone.

  • The Moat: Construction Industries revenue jumped 38%, and Power Generation sales (which includes data center backup power) surged 41%. The backlog is at record levels, and management described order activity as "strong."

  • The Opportunity: The company's Power & Energy segment is the hidden gem here. Data centers require massive backup power systems, and Caterpillar's large reciprocating engines and turbines are seeing accelerating demand. Meanwhile, the broader infrastructure spending cycle under the IIJA still has years of project timelines to work through.

  • Dividend Yield: 1.7%

  • Biggest Risk: Cyclicality. Caterpillar's business has always been tied to construction and mining cycles. A recession would hit demand, though the record backlog provides some cushion.

Vale (VALE)

Every chip, every cable, every transformer in every data center requires raw materials that somebody has to pull out of the ground. Vale is one of the largest mining companies on Earth, producing iron ore, nickel, and copper at a scale few competitors can match.

  • The Numbers: Vale currently trades at roughly 4.9x forward earnings with a dividend yield approaching 11%. Those are numbers you'd expect from a company in decline, not one sitting on critical materials for the energy transition and AI infrastructure buildout.

  • The Moat: Scale. Vale operates some of the largest and lowest-cost iron ore and nickel operations globally. Nickel demand is expected to grow substantially as battery production expands.

  • The Opportunity: Copper markets remain tight. EVs, grid infrastructure, and data centers all require copper, and new mine development takes a decade. Vale is positioned to benefit from any supply crunch.

  • Dividend Yield: ~11%

  • Biggest Risk: Commodity price volatility and geopolitical exposure. Vale is headquartered in Brazil, and its stock price often moves with iron ore prices and emerging market sentiment rather than company fundamentals.

Bottom Line

The AI revolution requires more than chips. It requires power infrastructure, heavy equipment, and raw materials at unprecedented scale. Eaton, Caterpillar, and Vale represent three different entry points into this physical buildout, each with distinct risk profiles and return potential.

Eaton offers the most direct AI infrastructure exposure but commands the highest valuation. Caterpillar provides diversified industrial exposure with a strong dividend and buyback program. Vale represents deep value with income, though it carries commodity and emerging market risk.

While everyone chases the next AI software play, these companies are executing on record backlogs with real revenue and real cash flow. Sometimes the boring trade is the smart trade.

Whatever Happened To…

For about 35 years, the most contested real estate in American pop culture measured roughly 8 inches by 7 inches: the side of a steel lunch box. From 1950 to 1985, whoever ruled the airwaves ruled the cafeteria, until plastic, economics, and worried parents brought the era clanging to a close.

The lunch box began as strictly blue-collar gear, a plain metal container for the working man. The revolution came in 1950, when Aladdin Industries stamped

TV cowboy Hopalong Cassidy on a steel box , the first lunch box to bear a licensed image. Sales jumped from 50,000 units to 600,000 in a single year.

Rival American Thermos fired back in 1953 with a full-color lithographed Roy Rogers box, and the arms race was on. For three decades, the lunch box served as a kid's first public declaration of identity: Davy Crockett or Zorro, the Beatles

or the Monkees, Star Trek or Star Wars .

Carrying last year's box was social suicide, which, of course, was the key to the whole business model. It was the Golden Age of illustrated metal lunch boxes.

By the late 1970s, though, the steel box had a target on its back. Schoolyard legend holds that mothers' groups, alarmed by kids swinging their boxes like medieval maces, lobbied to have metal lunch boxes banned as weapons, starting, the story goes, with Florida.

It was a perfect tale: the lunch box as both treasure and cudgel. It's also, apparently, a myth. No such Florida law was ever passed. The truth was duller and more decisive: plastic was simply cheaper, and by the early 1980s, the accountants had won the fight. The end came in 1985 with a Rambo box marking the final metal lunch box produced before safety worries and economics finished the switch to plastic.

Soft-sided nylon followed, and the steel pop-culture-branded lunch box retired to the collectibles market, where the very boxes Mom tossed out now fetch hundreds, sometimes thousands, at auction.
