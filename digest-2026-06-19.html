🦅 The Fed just stopped promising cuts.

For most of this year, the trade was simple: wait for the Federal Reserve to cut rates, and watch bonds and dividend stocks catch a bid. On Wednesday, in his first meeting as Fed Chair, Kevin Warsh ended that trade.

The Fed held its benchmark rate at 3.5% to 3.75% for the fourth straight time, in a unanimous vote, exactly as the market expected. With a decision this telegraphed, the number is never the story. What matters is what the new Chair does around it, and Warsh used his debut to send a message.

He scrapped the Fed's forward guidance entirely and declined to pencil in a rate projection of his own, a clean break from the Powell era and a sign this will be a quieter, more tight-lipped Fed that telegraphs less.

Then came the part markets were waiting on: the dot plot. The committee that spent all year sketching in a cut has flipped. Of the 18 officials who submitted projections, eight see no change this year, nine see at least one hike, and exactly one still sees a cut.

Warsh also raised the Fed's own inflation forecast to 3.6%, up from 2.7%, while trimming the growth outlook to 2.2%. Keep that projection separate from the actual print, which is hotter still: consumer prices rose 4.2% in May, the fastest in three years.

Translation for income investors: 

Stop waiting for cheaper money. It may not be coming.

Here’s what we're watching now. Earlier this year, the bet was that falling rates would lift bond prices and rate-sensitive dividend names. With the dots tilted toward a hike and no guidance to lean on, the steadier ground is locking in today's still-high yields rather than chasing a rate-cut-driven rally (which the Fed just warned against). Short-to-intermediate Treasuries, money-market funds, and T-bill ladders pay well to park your money in them, and dividend stocks can often ride out a higher-for-longer stretch. The next inflation print is the tell: another month near 4% and that one lonely rate cut vote starts to look very alone.

📰 Market Headlines

Stocks closed sharply lower Wednesday in a broad risk-off session. The Dow fell 507 points to 51,492, down 0.98%, while the S&P 500 lost 1.21% to 7,420 and the Nasdaq dropped 1.34% to 26,021. Big tech led the decline, with Microsoft down 3.8% to 378.85, and the VIX volatility gauge jumped more than 12% as traders moved to the sidelines. However, futures are pointing higher this morning, with chips leading a rebound and oil easing after the Iran deal.

On a day when almost everything closed red, one blue chip leaned into the income theme. Caterpillar rose 1.11% to lead the Dow, and the machinery maker is raising its quarterly dividend 8% to $1.63 a share, its 32nd straight year of increases as an S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrat. With rate cuts looking further off, durable dividend growers like Caterpillar are back in focus for income investors. The new payout is payable August 19 to holders of record July 20.

The chip trade that powers this whole rally is roaring. Intel has jumped about 11% this morning, extending Wednesday's pop, after President Trump said Apple agreed to use Intel's US foundry to make chips, a major vote of confidence on top of Intel moving its newest 18A-P manufacturing process into trial production. The strength is sector-wide: Broadcom is up around 4%, Micron roughly 6%, and AMD near 3.5%. Insatiable demand for semiconductors has underpinned the market's record run, and this morning it's leading the bounce.

Wholesale inflation ran hot in May. Producer prices rose 6.5% year over year, the fastest pace since November 2022, with energy doing most of the damage as wholesale gasoline jumped 23.4%. It's the same inflation backdrop now pushing the Fed to talk about hikes instead of cuts.

The SpaceX trade finally took a breather. Shares of the newly public rocket and AI venture fell nearly 5%, their first down day since the IPO, snapping a three-session surge that had carried the stock roughly 58% above its offering price. The pullback has not scared off the crowd: retail investors have bought $369.8 million of SPCX over the past three sessions, per Vanda Research, a pace with few precedents among recent market debuts.

Meta got a fresh reminder that its biggest risk may not be on the income statement. The stock slid 3.3% to $580.47 after a group of Italian families filed the country's first collective lawsuit against the company, alleging its recommendation algorithms harmed minors, including a 12-year-old girl who died by suicide. With more than 2,400 child-safety cases already pending in US federal court and a major trial ahead, investors are widening the legal-risk discount on the stock.

Robin Hood's Legendary Tree Dies

The

Major Oak in Sherwood Forest , the 1,200-year-old tree that legend says sheltered Robin Hood inside its hollow trunk, is believed to have died after failing to produce leaves this spring.

The Royal Society for the Protection of Birds said centuries of visitors

compacted the soil around the massive oak until it was as hard as concrete, strangling the root system and cutting off water and nutrients. The tree had been fenced off since the 1970s, but the damage was already deep.

Metal bracing, concrete fills, and structural props meant to save the tree likely made things worse, preventing it from aging naturally. Recent heatwaves and drought compounded the decline.

The oak will remain standing as a wildlife habitat and monument, and saplings from the tree have been planted around the world. A Hugh Jackman film,

🤨 What actually makes a dividend “safe” ?

With markets closed today for Juneteenth and the week's relief rally pulling oil and Treasury yields lower, we’re taking today as a strategy session. Specifically, we’re answering the question: where does durable dividend yield come from now?

Since 1960, about 85% of the S&P 500's total return has come from reinvested dividends and compounding, according to Hartford Funds. The yield is not a side dish. It is most of the meal.

The catch is that a high yield and a long history tell you almost nothing about whether a dividend survives the next downturn. Three numbers do most of the work.

First, the payout ratio: how much of earnings goes out as dividends. A range of 40% to 60% is generally healthy, because it leaves room to keep paying when profits dip. REITs and utilities run higher for legitimate reasons, so the bar is sector-specific.

Second, free-cash-flow coverage. Dividends are paid in cash, but earnings include non-cash items, so a company can show a comfortable payout ratio while cash flow barely covers the check. Analysts like to see free cash flow run comfortably above the dividend, and get nervous when it does not.

Third, the balance sheet. When a payout gets cut, debt is usually the trigger, because lenders get paid before shareholders. In 2022, AT&T reset its dividend from 2.08 to 1.11 dollars a share, a roughly 47% cut, after a debt-heavy acquisition left it carrying about 156 billion dollars in net debt. Investors who had chased its 8% headline yield ended up with a smaller dividend and a lower stock price. That is a yield trap, and all three numbers flashed before the cut.

That is why the screen and the math both matter. A Dividend King like Johnson & Johnson, raising its payout for a 64th straight year, has proven it through many recessions. But the streak is the starting point, not the proof. Run these three numbers yourself before you reach for the yield.

Cutting Edge AI Research
Beyond the Current Observation: Evaluating Multimodal Large Language Models in Controllable Non-Markov Games
This paper evaluates how well multimodal LLMs can plan and reason in game environments where past history matters (non-Markovian settings), testing their ability to look beyond immediate observations. It's significant because most LLM evaluations assume Markovian (memoryless) environments, so this reveals critical gaps in reasoning about sequential dependencies that are essential for real-world control tasks.
MolmoMotion: Forecasting Point Trajectories in 3D with Language Instruction
The paper develops a model that predicts 3D point trajectories in videos based on natural language instructions, combining vision and language for motion forecasting. This matters because it enables language-guided physical prediction—allowing users to specify what motion they want predicted in natural terms rather than through code or parameters.
Kairos: A Native World Model Stack for Physical AI
Kairos proposes an integrated architecture stack for building world models specifically designed for physical AI systems and robotic control. This is novel because it treats world modeling as a complete, cohesive system rather than isolated components, potentially enabling more reliable long-horizon planning for embodied agents.
The Reward Was in Your Data All Along: Correcting Flow Matching with Discriminator-Guided RL
This paper shows how to improve flow matching (a generative modeling technique) by using discriminator-guided reinforcement learning to extract better reward signals directly from training data. It's impactful because it bridges generative modeling and RL, potentially improving both sample efficiency and generation quality without requiring explicit reward engineering.
SAE Interventions are Unreliable: Post-Intervention Recovery of Suppressed Behavior
The paper demonstrates that Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), a popular mechanistic interpretability tool, don't reliably suppress model behaviors—suppressed capabilities can recover after intervention. This is critical for AI safety because it questions whether SAE-based interventions can actually control model behavior in practice.
