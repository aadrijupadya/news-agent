Hello.

Hedge funds, Congress members, and corporate insiders all trade in the same market you do. The difference is they have better information, faster access, and (in some cases) legal advantages that would get the rest of us investigated.

But here's what most people don't realize: almost everything they do is public. You just have to know where to look.

Today we're pulling back the curtain on how institutional money actually moves, how politicians trade, and how you can use the same data to make better decisions with your own portfolio.

This is not financial advice. Always do your own research.

How Hedge Funds Think (And Why You Should Care)

Hedge funds control a staggering amount of the market. Institutional investors account for roughly 80% of daily trading volume on the NYSE. Retail investors (that's us!) make up the rest. You're trading in their pool, not the other way around.

The biggest strategy to understand right now is the Treasury basis trade. Hedge funds have built trillions of dollars in leveraged positions exploiting tiny price differences between cash Treasuries and Treasury futures, with over $6 trillion flowing through repo and prime brokerage borrowing to fund the strategy. They use enormous leverage to make fractions of a penny profitable. When it works, they print money. When it doesn't (say, during a geopolitical shock), the unwinding can send shockwaves through the entire bond market.

Why does this matter for you? Because hedge funds now hold roughly 8% of the $31 trillion Treasury market. If something forces a mass unwind (and the current Iran situation is exactly the kind of catalyst that could), bond yields spike, borrowing costs rise, and stocks feel the pressure. Understanding this trade helps you understand why the bond market sometimes moves in ways that seem disconnected from the economy.

What you can actually track: Every hedge fund with over $100 million in assets has to file a 13F form with the SEC within 45 days of each quarter-end. That means you can see exactly what Buffett, Druckenmiller, and every other major fund manager bought and sold. The data is delayed (you're seeing positions from 45 days ago), but the directional signal is still valuable.

How Congress Trades (And Gets Away With It)

Before the STOCK Act passed in 2012, studies found that US Senators outperformed the S&P 500 by 12%. These are the same people voting on legislation that directly affects the companies they're trading!

The STOCK Act was supposed to fix this. It requires Congress members to disclose trades within 45 days. The penalty for not disclosing? A whopping… $200. And there have been zero prosecutions for violations since the law passed. So you can guess how well it's working.

That said, the disclosures are public. And tracking them has become an industry of its own.

A tool you can use right now:

AltIndex has its own Congress Trade Tracker that shows you new trade filings by date, Congress member, and ticker. It also shows the stock’s performance since the trade was executed, which can help you separate the more “skilled” Congressional traders from the bad ones.

We're not saying you should copy Congress members' trades blindly. But something like a senator on the Armed Services Committee buying defense stocks two weeks before a military authorization vote… that's the kind of signal worth paying attention to.

Insider Trading (The Legal Kind)

Corporate insiders (CEOs, CFOs, board members, major shareholders) are required to file a Form 4 with the SEC within 2 business days of buying or selling their own company's stock.

This is some of the most actionable public data in the market, and a lot of retail investors don't even know it exists.

When a CEO buys a million dollars of their own stock on the open market, they're telling you something. They know the company better than any analyst. They're putting their own money on the line. It doesn't guarantee the stock goes up, but a cluster of insider buys is one of the strongest bullish signals you can find.

Now, of course, CEOs will also sometimes buy a lot of their company’s stock as a form of posturing, hoping that the market thinks they have something up their sleeve. But it could just be a ruse! Still, if a CEO drops multiple millions of dollars on his own stock, we personally tend to believe that as a sign of conviction. People don’t tend to like to throw away millions of dollars.

That's all for today. The smart money isn't smarter than you. It just has better tools and faster data. Now you know where to find the same information they're using.

Whatever Happened To…

Chain letters are older than the internet, older than email, and nearly as old as modern mass mail itself. The recognizably modern chain letter took off in the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when cheap postage and improved delivery networks made it easy to copy a letter and send it along to friends.

The formula was simple and strangely compelling. Copy this message. Mail it to several people. Good fortune would follow if you did. Bad luck might follow if you did not.

The phenomenon exploded during the Great Depression with the famous "Send-a-Dime" letters of 1935. One widely circulated version,

called the Prosperity Club, asked recipients to mail a dime to the first name on a list, cross that name off, add their own at the bottom, and send copies to five friends. If the chain held, the math promised 15,625 dimes — $1,562.50, a small fortune at the time.

The craze became so intense that the Denver post office was handling tens of thousands of such letters a day at its peak, with single-day counts reaching 100,000 before postal inspectors cracked down. The math, of course, could never work: five recipients going just ten levels deep would already involve nearly ten million people.

When email spread in the 1990s, the chain letter adapted almost instantly. Forwarding email costs nothing and takes seconds, allowing the old format to scale dramatically. Inboxes filled with digital descendants: inspirational forwards, fake virus warnings, the endlessly recirculated plea for get-well cards to Craig Shergold (a real British boy who had long since recovered), and the infamous hoax promising that Bill Gates would pay you to forward an email.

Some were harmless. Others had a more practical purpose. Many early chain emails collected long strings of forwarded addresses in the header, which made them valuable tools for spammers building mailing lists.

Today, the classic postal chain letter is nearly extinct, and chain emails faded as spam filters, threaded inboxes, and the shift to social platforms quietly strangled them. But the underlying mechanism: copy this message and send it to others, is still alive and well in social media repost chains, TikTok and Facebook viral challenges, and messaging app hoaxes.

The Foundation: Index Funds (60% of the portfolio, $60,000)

Before you do anything fancy, you need a base. And the best base in investing is boring: a low-cost index fund that tracks the entire US stock market.

NOTE: These aren’t the allocations we think are “best” for everyone; remember, this is just meant to be a helpful starting point to consider. If you have way less index funds (or none), that’s fine, and we might even agree with you depending. Everyone has different risk tolerances.

We'd consider putting $60,000 into VTI (Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF) or VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF). Either works. VTI gives you exposure to the entire market (large, mid, and small cap). VOO gives you the S&P 500, which is the 500 largest companies only.

Why 60%? Because the S&P 500 has averaged about 10% annually over the last 30 years (and VTI at ~9% since inception)... while over 80% of actively managed funds fail to beat the S&P 500 over a 10-year period. You're not going to outsmart the market with your first $100,000. You're going to ride it.

This is the part of your portfolio that you don't touch. Let it compound. Let it grow. It will likely be able to do the heavy lifting for decades. A $60,000 investment growing at the historical average would be worth roughly $400,000 in 20 years and over $1,000,000 in 30. That's just math.

The Income Layer: Dividends (10-20% of the portfolio, $10,000-20,000)

The next $10,000 or $20,000 goes into something that pays you back while you hold it.

We'd split this between SCHD (Schwab US Dividend Equity ETF) and JEPI (JPMorgan Equity Premium Income ETF).

SCHD yields around 3.4% and holds 104 high-quality dividend-paying companies. It's the blue-chip dividend ETF that most income investors start with, and for good reason.

JEPI yields around 8% by selling covered calls on S&P 500 stocks. The trade-off is capped upside (we wrote a whole Saturday edition on this), but the monthly income is real and consistent. On $10,000 invested, JEPI pays roughly $800 a year, deposited monthly. $150,000 invested would get you $1,000 a month. Pretty neat math.

Why have an income layer this early? Because it teaches you the most powerful thing in investing: getting paid to hold. Once you see dividends hitting your account every month without selling a single share, your entire relationship with the market changes.

The Growth Bet: Sectors You Believe In (15-25% of the portfolio, $15,000-25,000)

Next, we’d take $15,000-25,000 and put it into some specific sectors we're genuinely bullish on long-term. For us, that's the AI, semiconductor, and energy buildout.

We’re talking SMH (VanEck Semiconductor ETF) or individual names like NVDA, AVGO, or TSM if you want to be more targeted. Then maybe nuclear energy names like Constellation Energy (CEG) and GE Vernova (GEV), alongside major uranium supplier Cameco Corp. (CCJ). The AI infrastructure cycle feels like a multi-year process to us. Companies are still pouring hundreds of billions into data centers and compute, and the energy grid is too small to handle current demand, so it will have to be built out. We think the companies making the chips and energy infrastructure underneath all of it will continue printing money long term.

But this slot doesn't have to be chips and energy. If you believe in space and defense, look at ITA or individual names like RKLB and LMT (or SpaceX this summer). If you’re big on Utilities, be our guest and invest in Dominion (D) or maybe Duke Energy (DUK). If you think gold is going to keep running (it's up over 44% in the last year alone), GLD is an option.

The point is: you're allowed to have a thesis. Just make sure you dip into multiple sectors, not a single stock yolo. Concentration builds wealth, but it can also destroy it. Maybe spread the risk across a few names or an ETF.

The Cash Reserve: Treasuries or Money Market (5% of the portfolio, $5,000)

The last $5,000 goes somewhere safe and liquid.

The 10-year Treasury is yielding 4.3%. Top money market funds like VMFXX are paying around 3.6%. I-Bonds are at 4.03%. All of these are dramatically better than the national average savings account rate of 0.38%.

This isn't exciting money. It's not supposed to be! It's the money that lets you sleep at night and not panic-sell your VTI when the market drops 10%. Every investor needs a cash buffer, even if it's small. The peace of mind is worth more than the yield.

You can also view these reserves as extra capital to deploy in market dips/corrections, but be careful: once you invest this cash, you won’t have any more left, even if the market keeps dipping. It could be best to leave it in treasuries or money market funds, or at least to dollar-cost average into a position over time.
