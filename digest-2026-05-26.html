5 American stocks  ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌ ‌

So today's exercise is simple. We picked five iconic American companies (Apple, Nvidia, Lockheed Martin, Johnson & Johnson, and Coca-Cola) and asked what $1,000 invested in each on Memorial Day 1996, 2006, and 2016 would be worth today, assuming dividends were reinvested. Nothing fancy… just good old-fashioned American investing.

The takeaway is highly encouraging: time in the market, plus a few good names, beats timing the market.

Apple: The Compounding Champion

Apple in 1996 was a struggling computer company. Steve Jobs hadn't even returned yet (that happened the following year). $1,000 in Apple stock back then felt closer to a charitable donation than an investment.

Three decades later, after multiple stock splits, the iPhone, the App Store, and 30 years of compounding, that same $1,000 (with dividends reinvested) would be worth somewhere over $1 million!

The 20-year holder did very well, too: somewhere around $130,000 from a $1,000 starting position in 2006. The 10-year holder is at about $12,000.

Can you believe that? A 12x in 10 years. And a 1000x in 30 years.

The reason Apple worked wasn't that it was the obvious pick (it wasn't in 1996 or even 2006). It worked in part because the people who held it didn't sell when it got scary. That includes the dot-com crash, 2008, the 2013 iPhone slowdown panic, the late-2018 Q4 wipeout, and the 2022 tech rout.

Nvidia: The Outlier That Almost Wasn't

If Apple is the slow build, Nvidia is the speedrun. The company went public in January 1999, so the 30-year holder doesn't exist here. But the 20-year and 10-year stories more than make up for it!

$1,000 in Nvidia on Memorial Day 2006 (when it was just a graphics chip company best known for selling GPUs to PC gamers) isworth somewhere in the neighborhood of $413,000 today.

$1,000 from Memorial Day 2016 (right after Nvidia's earliest AI-related earnings momentum started showing up) is worth roughly $200,000.

The absolute craziest part, though, is that Nvidia lost half its value multiple times along the way!

Nvidia is the closest thing this list has to a lottery ticket. It's also one of the cleanest examples of why you should keep some growth-stock exposure in their portfolios. A "safe" 60/40 balanced portfolio over the past 10 years would have produced roughly 150% total return. Nvidia produced something close to 200x.

Past performance does not predict future results. But ruling out high-growth names entirely because they're volatile is probably the wrong trade for most investors.

Lockheed Martin: The Steady Compounder

Nvidia's chart is what investors hope for. Lockheed Martin's is what they should actually expect for a best-case scenario from a well-run American business. The company was formed by merger in March 1995, so the 30-year holder caught it almost at the starting line.

$1,000 invested then, with dividends reinvested, is worth roughly $25,000 today. The 20-year holder is at about $12,000. The 10-year holder, about $3,000.

What stands out about Lockheed is the steadiness of the climb. Lockheed sells F-35s, missile defense systems, and rotary aircraft to the US military and to allied governments. That means demand doesn't move with the consumer cycle; it moves with the federal defense budget, which has trended meaningfully higher since the early 2000s.

Lockheed has raised its dividend every year for more than two decades running. It's not a Dividend Aristocrat yet (that title requires 25 consecutive years of raises), but it's close.

Johnson & Johnson: The Dividend Workhorse

Lockheed pays a respectable dividend. Johnson & Johnson takes dividends to a higher level. J&J is the textbook "boring American compounder," running pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and (until 2023) a big consumer health business that's now its own public company called Kenvue.

$1,000 in J&J on Memorial Day 1996, with dividends reinvested, is worth roughly $21,000 today on a total-return basis (which includes the value of the Kenvue separation in August 2023). The 20-year holder is at about $7,000. The 10-year holder, over $2,500.

The price appreciation is modest by tech standards. But the dividend is pretty attractive: J&J has raised its payout every year for more than 60 years straight. That makes it a Dividend King, the most exclusive club in income investing.

Coca-Cola: The Slow-and-Steady Surprise

J&J might be the textbook compounder, but Coca-Cola is the household name. It's also the surprise on this list. $1,000 in KO on Memorial Day 1996, with dividends reinvested, is worth somewhere around $9,000 today. 

Compare that to J&J ($21,000) over the same window. KO is the laggard of the list, and that's the lesson.

The reason: Coca-Cola spent roughly a decade going sideways. KO peaked above $80 a share in 1998, then traded mostly flat through the mid-2000s as the company worked through changing consumer tastes and slowing soda volumes. By May 2006, the share price was almost the same where it had been in May 1996. Patient holders eventually got paid in dividends (KO is a 64-year Dividend King), but they didn't get the headline price chart of an Apple or an Nvidia.

If you invested the $1,000 on Memorial Day 20 years ago, you’d have roughly $6,000. And on Memorial Day 2016, you’d have about $2,200. 

The takeaway: even iconic American companies can have decade-long stretches of going nowhere. Holding through that is hard. Holders who did, and reinvested their dividends along the way, still finished with roughly a 9x on their money.

The Throughline

Five companies, three time horizons. The pattern is hard to miss:

The companies that produced outsize returns (Apple, Nvidia) required holders to sit through brutal drawdowns. Nvidia has lost more than half its value on at least four separate occasions. Apple lost more than 80% during the dot-com bust before its turnaround.

The boring-but-steady names (Lockheed, J&J) did what they were supposed to do: compound at a respectable pace, pay growing dividends, and not test your conviction too badly along the way.

The lagger on the list (Coca-Cola) is the reminder that "iconic" doesn't automatically mean "great long-term return." The dividend rescued the trade. The price chart alone would have tested the patience of any holder who wasn't getting paid quarterly to wait.

The through line applies them all: time in the market, plus a willingness to hold through pain, beats timing the market.

1. Ro Khanna (D-CA): Strong Buys, Mistimed Sells

Ro Khanna represents Silicon Valley, so his disclosures tend to attract extra attention. His recent trades show him buying broadly across big tech companies and adding JPMorgan, while selling some former winners. His buys have been well-timed… His sells, less so.

The buys include Amazon (AMZN), Microchip Technology (MCHP), JPMorgan Chase & Co (JPM). The sells include Alphabet (GOOGL), Micron (MU), and Nasdaq Inc. (NDAQ)

The buys doing particularly well include Microchip Technology (up over 35%) and Amazon (up over 20%), though JPM is in a slight slump. Unfortunately for Khanna though, his sells all rallied after he exited. Micron alone is up more than 40% since Khanna sold in late April on AI memory demand!

What it tells you: Khanna's buys may be worth a second look. His sells aren't a reliable exit signal. It might be interesting to use his future disclosures to find names to pay attention to, but probably not as a signal to take profits.

2. Byron Donalds (R-FL): A Well-Timed Marvell Buy

Byron Donalds, the Florida congressman, filed several April 2 trades that included a buy of Marvell Technology (MRVL). Since the trade, MRVL is up nearly 77%.

Marvell designs custom AI chips and the networking silicon that connects data centers together. The company has been one of the quieter beneficiaries of the AI infrastructure buildout, providing custom silicon for hyperscaler customers like Amazon, Microsoft, and Google. The buy timing on this one looks exceptionally well-positioned.

Donalds also bought Eli Lilly (LLY) the same day, which is about where he bought it at price wise. 

3. Chip Roy (R-TX): The Biggest Disclosure (With a Twist)

Texas Republican Chip Roy received between $5 million and $25 million in AESI, an “energy-sector solutions provider” to the oil and natural gas industry, on April 30. This is the largest single position-size disclosure in the recent batch. Notably, he’d sold the same stock about two years prior.

NOTE: you may have noticed we said “received,” not “bought.” That’s because the AESI stock was awarded to Roy's wife as part of her compensation; she works for the company. So, this was not a conviction buy. It was family exposure through employment.

4. Gilbert Cisneros (D-CA): The Active Portfolio Manager

Gilbert Cisneros is the most active filer in the recent disclosures, with roughly 50 trades across the latest batch. The mix looks like a quarterly portfolio rebalance rather than a series of one-name conviction bets.

The standout buys timed well: Advanced Micro Devices (AMD, up over 70% since) and Eli Lilly (LLY, up 7%). The notable sells were mistimed in the other direction: CrowdStrike (CRWD, up 61%), Fortinet (FTNT, up 62%), and Flex (FLEX, up 42%).

The pattern is interesting. Cisneros bought into some rising momentum names (AMD, LLY) and sold into others (CRWD, FTNT, FLEX). For our two cents, that seems more like portfolio churn rather than a timing strategy. Still maybe useful as a directional sign for which sectors he sees as overheated versus still running.

5. John Boozman (R-AR): A Patient Alphabet Bet

Arkansas Republican John Boozman has been a relatively quiet trader. One of his September 2025 disclosures has aged exceptionally well, though: a buy of Alphabet (GOOGL) at around $249 per share on May 20, 2025. The stock is up roughly 110% since. He bought again on September 17 of 2025.

The timing was the strong part. Boozman bought right as Google's Gemini momentum was picking up and again right as the antitrust pressure that had been weighing on the stock began to ease.
