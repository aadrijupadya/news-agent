Hello.

Most people picture their individual retirement account (IRA) holding index funds, target-date funds, and maybe some individual stocks. But the IRS lets you hold a lot more inside an IRA than most investors realize. We're talking real estate, private credit, gold, Bitcoin, private equity, and even startup shares.

Once you understand the structure, you can use the same tax-advantaged wrapper that holds your S&P 500 ETF to hold the kinds of investments that most retail investors never associate with retirement accounts.

Here's how it works.

As always, this is not financial advice. Always do your own research. Past performance doesn't guarantee future results.

What a Self-Directed IRA Actually Is

Quick definition: a self-directed IRA (SDIRA) is a regular IRA held at a custodian that allows you to invest in things beyond publicly traded securities. 

The IRS doesn't have a "self-directed" designation. So, as far as the law is concerned, all IRAs are self-directed in the sense that you choose what's inside them. The difference is whether your custodian will allow you to invest in what you want.

Both flavors are available:

  • Traditional SDIRA: tax-deferred contributions, taxed at withdrawal

  • Roth SDIRA: after-tax contributions, fully tax-free at qualified withdrawal

Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard mostly stick to stocks, ETFs, mutual funds, and public bonds. They aren't set up to hold a deed to a rental property or shares of a private company. However, some specialized SDIRA custodians like Equity Trust, Alto, IRA Financial, and Rocket Dollar are.

So if the IRS rules are broader than most people realize, what exactly can you put inside one of these accounts?

What CAN’T You Hold in an SDIRA?

Rather than spelling out every permitted investment, the IRS mainly focuses on what’s off-limits. A couple of restricted items include:

  • Life insurance contracts.

  • Property you directly benefit from. This includes living on the property (though you can hold other real estate in an IRA though).

  • Collectibles. This includes art, antiques, gems, stamps, alcoholic beverages, most coins, and many other things. There's a carve-out for certain U.S.-minted bullion meeting strict purity standards.

The most popular alternative assets investors actually use in an IRA

Aside from the above and a couple of other restrictions, everything else is on the table:

  • Real estate. Rental properties, commercial buildings, and raw land.

  • Private credit and promissory notes. Your IRA acts as the lender, and all interest income flows back tax-advantaged.

  • Private equity and startup shares. Including founder shares of pre-IPO companies.

  • Precious metals. Must be held at an IRS-approved depository, not your home safe.

  • Cryptocurrency. Bitcoin, Ethereum, and others, with keys held by your custodian.

  • Tax liens, mineral rights, oil and gas royalties. Niche but allowed

Translation: your IRA can hold most of what a hedge fund holds, even if it can’t trade like one.

The Strategies Most People Don't Know Are Allowed

Three plays that are dramatically underused:

The Roth real estate compounder. Buy rental property inside a Roth SDIRA and every dollar of rental income plus every dollar of appreciation comes out tax-free at retirement. No depreciation recapture, no capital gains, no income tax on the rents. The trade-off: any leverage triggers a separate tax (more on that in a moment).

The Peter Thiel. In 1999, Peter Thiel bought roughly 1.7 million founder shares of PayPal at $0.001 per share for about $1,700 inside his Roth IRA. By 2019, that position was worth about $5 billion, according to Pro Publica. The play is legal in principle (capitalizing a startup with Roth dollars), provided you buy at fair market value rather than a sweetheart price that benefits you as the fiduciary.

The Mega Backdoor Roth (via Solo 401(k)). If you're self-employed with no full-time employees, a properly drafted Solo 401(k) plan can let you contribute up to $72,000 in 2026, much of it as after-tax Roth dollars via the "mega backdoor" route. 

The Landmines

This is where SDIRAs get expensive fast.

The IRS publishes a list of "prohibited transactions" that we’ve already covered. And stepping on one isn't a slap on the wrist. The penalty for breaking one of these rules: your entire IRA loses tax-advantaged status as of January 1 of the year you broke the rule. The full balance becomes ordinary income, plus a 10% early-withdrawal penalty if you're under 59½.

Common ways people blow up their SDIRA:

  • Self-dealing. You can't live in a property your IRA owns. The same goes for your spouse, kids, parents, or grandparents. Letting any "disqualified person" use IRA-owned property is a violation.

  • Lending to family. You can lend IRA money to a stranger, your sibling, or your cousin. You can't lend to your spouse, parent, child, or grandchild.

  • Paying yourself. In Ellis v. Commissioner (2015), an IRA owner ran a used-car business through an IRA-owned LLC and paid himself a salary. The salary was a prohibited transaction. His entire IRA was disqualified.

  • Holding gold at home. In McNulty v. Commissioner (2021), a couple stored about $411,000 of American Eagle gold coins in a home safe through an IRA-owned LLC. The Tax Court ruled that physical possession by the IRA owner violates the trustee-custody rule. The whole position was deemed distributed.

So you now know what’s prohibited, what’s permitted, and the potential flags to watch for… But is this strategy actually right for you?

Who This Is Actually For

A few practical filters if you’re considering this kind of an IRA:

  • You should have a specific alternative-asset thesis. Opening an SDIRA "in case you find something" is usually a bad call, since annual custodian fees (~$200–$500) eat returns on small or inactive accounts.

  • You should be comfortable with illiquidity. Real estate or private equity inside an IRA can be hard to value, sell, or convert to cash for required minimum distributions at retirement.

  • You should have either the patience for paperwork or the budget to pay someone who does.

For anyone with an aggressive investing lean or with a multi-decade time horizon, the Roth real estate compounder and startup equity plays are where SDIRAs earn their keep. Pairing aggressive long-term assets with the most tax-advantaged wrapper available is closer to a hedge fund structure than most retail accounts ever get.

Bottom Line

A self-directed IRA gives you access to most of the alternative assets sophisticated investors use (real estate, private credit, startup equity, gold, crypto) inside the most tax-advantaged wrapper the IRS offers. Done well, particularly in a Roth, the upside compounds tax-free for decades.

Done badly, you can disqualify your entire IRA in a single transaction. The rules are not forgiving and the penalty is total!

If the appeal is strong, talk to a tax attorney or CPA who's actually done SDIRA work before, then pick a reputable custodian, then bring a specific thesis. The IRS gives you the tools. It's on you to use them carefully.

Inside Leopold Aschenbrenner's $14 Billion AI Power Bet

Leopold Aschenbrenner graduated from Columbia as valedictorian at 19 with degrees in economics and math-statistics. He joined OpenAI's Superalignment team in 2023 and was fired from OpenAI in April 2024 (he says it was over an internal security memo; OpenAI says it was an information leak). A few months later, he published "Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead," a sprawling essay laying out his belief that AGI could arrive by 2027 and that the second half of the AI race would be won by whoever controls the most electricity, not whoever builds the best chips.

Then he started trading on it.

His fund, Situational Awareness LP grew from $225 million to $5.5 billion in about 12 months. By March 31, 2026, the fund's disclosed positions totaled $13.7 billion, with returns exceeding 140% year-to-date in early 2026.

The short book is massive. Aschenbrenner holds $8.46 billion in put options against Nvidia, Broadcom, AMD, the VanEck Semiconductor ETF, Oracle, and more. That's more than half the portfolio betting against the biggest names in chips.

The long side tells you where he thinks the value is moving, though. He owns large positions in bitcoin mining companies (IREN, CORZ, RIOT, CLSK, BITF, BTDR, and HIVE), plus $878.7 million in Bloom Energy, $724.4 million in SanDisk, and $556.1 million in CoreWeave.

The thesis connecting all of it: bitcoin miners have already contracted massive amounts of power capacity that can be redirected to AI data centers. They also have the data centers and compute already. So Aschenbrenner isn't buying miners for the mining. He's buying them because they’re pivoting into AI, and they hold the most important resources for the buildout.

It also works as a barbell hedge. If Nvidia crushes earnings today, the puts lose value but the infrastructure longs rally because a strong Nvidia quarter confirms the AI buildout is accelerating (which means more demand for power). If Nvidia misses, the puts print.

We're not saying you should go short Nvidia. But we think Aschenbrenner's framing is worth paying attention to: the first phase of the AI trade was about who makes the chips. The next phase looks to be about who controls the power.

The Case for Asymmetric Bets (and 10 Micro Caps the Data Likes)

There's a version of investing advice that says you should never touch a stock under $5. And for most people, most of the time, that's fine advice. Micro caps are volatile, thinly traded, and more likely to go to zero than to the moon.

But we think writing them off completely is a mistake.

Not because we think you should throw 20% of your portfolio into penny stocks. That's how you blow up an account. We're talking about 1% to 5%, max. Money you could light on fire and still sleep fine. That's the starting line for asymmetric bets.

The concept is simple: you're risking a small amount for the chance at an outsized return. You're not betting the house. You're buying a few shots in the chamber, except these shots come with quarterly earnings reports, SEC filings, and CEO interviews you can go watch yourself.

That's what separates micro cap investing. These are public companies. They report their financials just like Apple and Nvidia. You can pull up the balance sheet, look at revenue trends, check insider buying, and make your own call on whether the business has a shot.

The other piece most people miss: you should probably spread your investments around. For example, if you've set aside 3% of your portfolio for small cap bets, putting all of it on one ticker is closer to gambling than investing. Split it across 3, 5, or even 10 names. Some will go to zero. Some will go sideways. But if one or two hit, the math can be worth it.

We're not going to lie, this stuff isn't for everyone. If this level of volatility makes your stomach turn, we totally understand (and you can skip right to the market headlines below). Cutting Edge AI Research
Active Learners as Efficient PRP Rerankers
This paper repurposes active learning strategies to improve passage ranking in information retrieval by treating reranking as a selective labeling problem. The approach is novel because it uses uncertainty-based active learning to identify which passages most benefit from reranking, reducing computational costs while maintaining retrieval quality.
When Vision Speaks for Sound
The paper proposes using visual information from videos to improve or generate audio understanding without relying on paired audio data. This is significant because it bridges the vision-audio modality gap through self-supervised learning, enabling audio tasks in scenarios where sound is unavailable or corrupted.
Anti-Self-Distillation for Reasoning RL via Pointwise Mutual Information
This work introduces a novel training objective for reinforcement learning in reasoning tasks that prevents models from over-relying on their own learned patterns through a pointwise mutual information penalty. The approach is important because it directly addresses reward hacking and distribution collapse in RL-based reasoning, improving solution diversity and robustness.
OpenComputer: Verifiable Software Worlds for Computer-Use Agents
OpenComputer provides a controlled simulation environment where computer-use agents can be trained and evaluated with ground-truth verification of task completion. This addresses a critical gap in agent benchmarking by offering reproducible, verifiable evaluation without relying on fragile vision-based success metrics.
GoLongRL: Capability-Oriented Long Context Reinforcement Learning with Multitask Alignment
This paper extends RL training to handle long-context reasoning tasks by aligning model capabilities across multiple tasks and maintaining coherent behavior over extended sequences. The work is significant because long-context reasoning in RL remains underexplored, and this multitask alignment approach could improve generalization in complex agent scenarios.
