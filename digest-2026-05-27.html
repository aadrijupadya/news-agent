Hello.

Every individual investor knows the rule about market crashes: don't sell, ride it out, you'll be fine eventually. Most of us nod along and then panic-sell anyway when our retirement balance drops 20% or 30% in a couple of months.

The three-bucket strategy is one of the cleanest fixes for that problem.

Today's edition covers how to set it up, why it works, and how to actually deploy it the next time the market does something ugly. The premise is simple: split your portfolio into three buckets tied to different time horizons. When markets crash, you spend from the short-term bucket and leave the long-term bucket alone to recover. That's the whole framework. Most of the work is in setting it up correctly and resisting the urge to mess with it.

The Three Buckets, Explained Plainly

The strategy splits your investable assets into three buckets, each tied to when you'd actually need the money.

Bucket 1 (Short-term: 1 to 2 years of expenses). Cash, high-yield savings, or short-term Treasury funds. The money you'd live on for the next year or two if your income vanished tomorrow. This bucket doesn't grow much and isn't trying to. Its job is to be there when you need it, even if the stock market is in shambles.

Bucket 2 (Medium-term: 3 to 10 years). Mostly short- and intermediate-term bonds, with some balanced funds or dividend-paying stocks for modest growth. The money you don't need immediately but might want in five-ish years. It grows at a respectable pace and rides shorter market downturns without too much drama.

Bucket 3 (Long-term: 10+ years). Growth-oriented stocks, the S&P 500 index, sector ETFs like the ones we covered in last week's stock-vs-ETF edition. The money you genuinely don't touch for at least a decade. This bucket carries the volatility, and it's also where most of your long-term return comes from.

The amount that goes in each bucket depends on your stage of life. Christine Benz at Morningstar publishes a widely cited "aggressive" version of this approach that's roughly 8% in Bucket 1, 32% in Bucket 2, and 60% in Bucket 3. Someone earlier in their career can run Bucket 3 even higher, since longer time horizons absorb more volatility comfortably.

Why This Strategy Actually Works

The whole point of the bucket setup is to help you ride out something called sequence of returns risk. In plain English: the biggest threat to your retirement balance is having a market crash hit right when you need to start withdrawing money.

If you're forced to sell stocks at the bottom of a crash to cover living expenses, you lock in those losses permanently. Even worse, you reduce the number of shares you own going into the recovery. That damage compounds: your portfolio takes years longer to bounce back than it would have if you'd just left it alone.

The catch? Crashes always come, and most of them don't announce themselves. The S&P 500 has had about ten bear markets (drops of 20% or more) since 1950. The average one took roughly 9 to 10 months to hit the bottom and another year and a half to two years to fully recover. If one of those hits when you're a few years from retirement and you don't have any cash on hand, you're forced to sell at the worst possible time.

The three-bucket setup decides that question for you in advance. Spend from Bucket 1, leave Buckets 2 and 3 alone, and time does the rest.

How to Use Your Buckets When the Market Crashes

Setting the buckets up is the easy part. Using them correctly when the market is in freefall is where most individual investors fall down.

When the market drops 20% or more:

  • Cover your living expenses out of Bucket 1. That's literally what it's there for. Do not touch Bucket 3.

  • If you have spare cash beyond what you need for living expenses, use it to buy more of Bucket 3 while it's on sale. The Nvidia 2022 drawdown (down about 50% peak to trough) is a textbook example. The investors who bought during that drop made multiples on their money over the next two years.

  • If Bucket 1 starts running low, refill it from Bucket 2, not Bucket 3. Bucket 3 needs time to recover. Bucket 2 will probably hold up better in most downturns.

When the market recovers:

  • As you take profits, you can refill Bucket 1 (if needed) from Buckets 2 and 3 to bring them back toward your target split. This is a rebalancing move, not a market call.

  • You can also just refill Bucket 1 from your income (this would be ideal, but if you don’t have income, not a problem).

The whole point of this is that you're using the bucket structure to systematically buy low and sell high without having to make any heroic forecasting calls.

Some Common Mistakes

Plenty of investors set the buckets up correctly, then let them drift back into a single pile of holdings over the years. A few things to watch for:

  • Bucket 1 sitting in a near-zero-yield checking account. A best high-yield savings account or short-term Treasury fund pays roughly 4% (or even 5%) these days. Letting that money earn 0.01% in a regular checking account costs you real money over time.

  • Treating Bucket 2 as "the safe one." Bucket 2 still has equity exposure. In a bad enough crash, it can drop 15 to 20%. Plan around that. Don't be surprised by it.

  • Pulling from Bucket 3 in a panic anyway. This is the single biggest failure mode. If you don't actually believe in the rules, the bucket setup is just a complicated way to draw the same panicked lines you would have drawn without it.

  • Never refilling Bucket 1. After a long bull market, lots of investors find their portfolio has drifted into Bucket 3 by default. That's fine until the next crash arrives and you're back to having no cash on hand.

The AI Chip Ecosystem (And Why All the Money Is Going to Asia)

If you want to understand where the money is going in AI, you need to understand how a single Nvidia (NVDA) GPU gets built, powered, connected, and put to work. The key is that it takes an entire supply chain to do it, and most of it runs through Asia.

The designer: Nvidia designs the GPU. The company is spending roughly $150 billion per year in Taiwan on a new campus, up from $10 to $15 billion just four or five years ago.

The manufacturer: TSMC (TSM) of Taiwan builds the chips Nvidia designs. Without TSMC's fabrication, Nvidia's blueprints are just blueprints. This is why Taiwan is potentially the most important island in the global economy right now.

The memory: Every AI GPU needs high-bandwidth memory (HBM) to function. That's where South Korea’s SK Hynix (KRX: 000660) and Samsung (KRX: 005930) come in. SK Hynix just joined the $1 trillion market cap club, with shares up roughly 250% since the start of the year. It's a key HBM supplier for Nvidia. And Samsung is investing $1.5 billion in a new chip testing plant in Vietnam to address the DRAM and NAND shortage. And Micron (MU), the US-based memory giant, rocketed 19% after UBS nearly tripled its price target from $535 to $1,625, pushing its own market cap past $1 trillion for the first time.

The networking: Once the GPUs are built, you need chips that move data between them at massive scale. That's Marvell Technology (MRVL) ,which reports earnings today AND is our AltIndex stock of the day (below). Broadcom (AVGO) sits in this category, too. Jensen Huang invested $2 billion into Marvell for exactly this reason.

The cloud: Then you need someone to rack the GPUs, power them, and rent the compute. IREN just signed a $1.6 billion deal with Dell for Blackwell systems, targeting $4.4 billion in annualized revenue. CoreWeave (CRWV) and Nebius (NBIS) are doing the same thing.

The customers: OpenAI, Anthropic, ByteDance, and every hyperscaler on Earth.

Here's the takeaway for your portfolio: Nvidia gets all the headlines, but the companies surrounding it (the memory suppliers, the networking chipmakers, the cloud providers) are where some of the most explosive growth is happening right now. SK Hynix is up 312% the past six months. Micron just hit $1 trillion. Marvell is up 133% year to date. If you only own Nvidia, you're playing the AI trade with one hand tied behind your back.

A heads up: we’ve been at all-time highs for a long time now. DVAO: Dynamic Variance-adaptive Advantage Optimization for Multi-reward Reinforcement Learning
This paper introduces a new optimization technique that dynamically adjusts how RL agents balance multiple competing reward signals by adapting variance estimates during training. It matters because most real-world RL problems involve conflicting objectives (e.g., speed vs. safety), and this method provides a principled way to handle them without manual tuning.
WBench: A Comprehensive Multi-turn Benchmark for Interactive Video World Model Evaluation
WBench provides the first large-scale benchmark for evaluating video world models—AI systems that predict future video frames—with interactive, multi-turn scenarios that reflect real-world usage. This is significant because existing evaluations are limited to passive, single-prediction tasks, while this benchmark tests whether models can maintain consistency across multiple agent interactions over time.
Macaron-A2UI: A Model for Generative UI in Personal Agents
This paper presents a generative model that can create custom user interfaces on-the-fly for personal AI agents, adapting layouts and interactions to different tasks and users. It matters because it moves beyond fixed UI templates toward truly personalized agent interfaces, addressing a key bottleneck in making agents feel responsive to individual needs.
QUEST: Training Frontier Deep Research Agents with Fully Synthetic Tasks
QUEST demonstrates how to train AI agents to solve complex research problems using only synthetically-generated tasks, without requiring real labeled research data. This is novel because it shows that agents can learn genuine problem-solving capabilities from carefully constructed synthetic environments, potentially accelerating AI-assisted scientific discovery.
Foundation Protocol: A Coordination Layer for Agentic Society
This paper proposes a protocol standard for enabling multiple AI agents to coordinate, communicate, and collaborate with each other at scale.
